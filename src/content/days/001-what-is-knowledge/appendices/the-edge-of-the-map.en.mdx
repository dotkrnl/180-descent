## Epistemology forgot to ask about *inquiry*

<StatusChipRow><StatusChip status={"hint"} label={"The puzzle is real · taken seriously"} printLabel={"promising"} /><StatusChip status={"bad"} label={"\"all norms are zetetic\" · contested"} printLabel={"contested"} /></StatusChipRow>
Much of twentieth-century epistemology focused on states such as belief, justification, and knowledge. It paid less attention to the activity that produces them: *inquiry* — asking a question, deciding what evidence to gather, and knowing when to stop. Jane Friedman calls the norms of inquiry <Term as="em">zetetic</Term> norms (from the Greek *zētein*, to seek). Her paper "The Epistemic and the Zetetic" (*Philosophical Review*, 2020) argues that the norms of *inquiry* and the norms of *belief* can conflict.

The <Term as="em">Zetetic Instrumental Principle</Term> says that if you want to answer a question, you should take the means needed to answer it. This can conflict with the evidentialist permission to believe whatever your evidence already supports. Suppose you are counting the windows on a building across the street. Good inquiry tells you to focus on the windows. Meanwhile, your senses support many unrelated truths — the color of a car, the number of people on the corner, the shape of a cloud. The belief norm permits those beliefs; the inquiry norm tells you to ignore them for now. The diagram makes the conflict concrete.

<AppendixFigure variant="wide">
<ZeteticNormTensionFigure locale="en" />
<Caption class="figcap">Friedman's tension: good inquiry and permissible belief pull opposite ways.</Caption>
</AppendixFigure>

If belief norms and inquiry norms genuinely clash, then a theory built only on belief is incomplete. The stronger "zetetic turn" holds that **all epistemic norms are ultimately norms of inquiry**: suspension of judgment becomes a question-directed attitude, while believing an answer closes a question. Arianna Falbo ("Should epistemology take the zetetic turn?", *Philosophical Studies*, 2023) and others object that inquiry norms may be practical rather than distinctively epistemic, and that a purely zetetic account cannot explain why some inquiry-helping beliefs remain irrational. The conflict is taken seriously; the stronger thesis remains unresolved. Either way, "what is knowledge?" now sits beside "what is it to inquire well?" That second question leads directly to **Day 2**, where scientific method supplies norms for collective inquiry.

## What if knowledge comes *before* belief?

<StatusChipRow><StatusChip status={"hint"} label={"The empirical pattern · fairly robust"} printLabel={"promising"} /><StatusChip status={"bad"} label={"\"knowledge is more basic\" · contested"} printLabel={"contested"} /></StatusChipRow>
Day 1 treated knowledge as something *built up from* belief: take a belief, add truth, add justification, and screen out luck. The theories we met generally assumed belief is the raw material and knowledge the finished product. In the *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* target article "Knowledge before belief" (2021), an interdisciplinary team led by Jonathan Phillips and Joshua Knobe argues that human and animal cognition may develop in the opposite order.

The standard story in psychology is that our "theory of mind" — our capacity to model other minds — is centered on *belief*, and matures when a child finally passes the *false-belief test* (understanding that someone can hold a belief the child knows to be false) around age four. Phillips and colleagues marshal converging evidence that representing *knowledge* is the more basic feat. The threads: developmentally, infants and toddlers track who has *seen* or has *access to* information — who *knows* — well before they can handle false beliefs. Comparatively, non-human great apes show robust signs of tracking what others can perceive and know, while convincing evidence of belief-tracking remains elusive. And in adults, attributions of knowledge are made at least as fast as — sometimes faster than — attributions of belief, which is hard to explain if "X knows p" is computed by first building "X believes p" and then checking extra conditions. The picture they paint is a <Term as="em">factive theory of mind</Term>: the mind's first and most basic tool for modeling others is "what do they know?", with the trickier, error-tolerant "what do they merely *believe* (perhaps falsely)?" coming later and costing more.

<AppendixFigure variant="wide">
<KnowledgeBeforeBeliefTimeline locale="en" />
<Caption class="figcap">The proposed ordering — the reverse of the textbook "belief-first" story.</Caption>
</AppendixFigure>

If it holds, this would give empirical support to Timothy Williamson's *knowledge-first* program — the philosophical claim that knowledge is basic and belief should be explained in terms of it, not the reverse. It would also have an evolutionary rationale: a social animal benefits from tracking who has reliable information, echoing Edward Craig's "good-informant" genealogy from Appendix I. The target article prompted dozens of peer commentaries, many dissenting. Critics argue that the knowledge/belief line is blurrier than the authors allow, that tracking who saw what need not amount to representing *knowledge*, and that culture and language shape the pattern. The behavioral findings — children and apes track informational access early — are reasonably solid. The stronger interpretation, in which knowledge representation is metaphysically and computationally prior to belief, remains disputed.

## Does a language model know anything — or just bullshit?

<StatusChipRow><StatusChip status={"ok"} label={"Next-token training does not guarantee truth · established"} printLabel={"established"} /><StatusChip status={"bad"} label={"\"truth-indifferent\" diagnosis · contested"} printLabel={"contested"} /></StatusChipRow>
Day 1 ended with a question: when a language model outputs a true, well-supported sentence, does it *know* anything — or is it right for reasons that do not connect it to the truth in the way knowledge requires? One influential answer is Hicks, Humphries, and Slater's deliberately provocative "ChatGPT is bullshit" (*Ethics and Information Technology*, 2024).

They borrow Harry Frankfurt's technical sense of <Term as="em">bullshit</Term> (from his 1986 essay *On Bullshit*): speech produced with *indifference* to truth. A liar must track the truth in order to conceal it; a bullshitter need not. Autoregressive language models are pretrained to predict the next <Term as="em">token</Term><TipNote text={"A token is the unit of text a language model predicts; it can be a whole word, a word piece, punctuation, or spacing."} />. That objective rewards fit to the training distribution, but it does not by itself require every generated claim to be true. Hicks and colleagues argue that this gap makes "bullshit" more accurate than "hallucination": the latter sounds like an accidental malfunction, while fluent truth and fluent falsehood can both arise from the same generation procedure. They distinguish *soft* bullshit (no intent to deceive, only truth-indifference) from *hard* bullshit (also posing as a sincere truth-teller), and place language models in at least the first category.

The diagnosis challenges loose talk of machines "knowing," "understanding," or "believing," but it is contested. Some critics argue that "bullshit" presupposes a position on whether models have intentions at all (Sarah Fisher, "Large language models and their big bullshit potential," 2024; David Gunkel &amp; Simon Coghlan, "Cut the crap," 2025). Empirical results also complicate the simplest version of truth-indifference: model confidence and internal states can sometimes discriminate correct from incorrect answers, even though that information is communicated poorly to users, while separate benchmarks find that models still reason unreliably about the distinction between belief, knowledge, and fact. None of this establishes that a model *knows*. It does show why "no representation of truth" is too strong. The settled point is narrower: next-token pretraining alone supplies no truth guarantee, and fluency is not evidence of knowledge. Whether a model's output is best understood as bullshit, an instrument reading, testimony, or some new epistemic category remains open.

## The support beam you never noticed: the epistemic backstop

<StatusChipRow><StatusChip status={"hint"} label={"The mechanism · plausible & influential"} printLabel={"promising"} /><StatusChip status={"bad"} label={"\"epistemic apocalypse\" · contested"} printLabel={"contested"} /></StatusChipRow>
Appendix I established how much of what you know arrives by *testimony* — other people's say-so. Regina Rini's "Deepfakes and the Epistemic Backstop" (*Philosophers' Imprint*, 2020) identifies a hidden structural support that has quietly kept that whole edifice credible — and shows how a new technology is sawing through it.

The insight is subtle. Why is testimony as reliable as it is? Part of the answer, Rini argues, is a silent regulator: the ever-present *possibility* of a recording. When someone can be contradicted by a photograph, an audio clip, or a video, they have a standing incentive to testify truthfully — because a recording might surface and catch them out. Recordings function as an <Term as="em">epistemic backstop</Term><TipNote text={"An epistemic backstop is a source of possible checking that disciplines testimony even when no one checks it constantly."} />: not because we constantly check them, but because their mere availability disciplines testimony, the way an unused referee still shapes a game. For roughly a century — since photography and audio became hard to fake — we have built our norms of public truth-telling on this backstop without ever naming it. Deepfakes — convincingly fabricated video and audio, generated by the same machine-learning wave as §3 — dissolve it from both sides. They flood the channel with convincing fakes *and*, just as corrosively, they hand every caught wrongdoer a new escape: *that recording of me is probably a deepfake.* The "<Term as="em">liar's dividend</Term>"<TipNote text={"The liar’s dividend is the benefit liars get when real evidence can be dismissed as fake."} />. Once any recording can be waved away, the backstop stops disciplining testimony — and testimony itself, our single largest source of knowledge, loses a support we didn't know it leaned on. Don Fallis sharpens the same worry in information-theoretic terms ("The Epistemic Threat of Deepfakes," *Philosophy &amp; Technology*, 2021): deepfakes *reduce the information* a video carries about what actually happened, degrading it as a signal.

<AppendixFigure variant="wide">
<EpistemicBackstopFigure locale="en" />
<Caption class="figcap">Knock out a support no one was looking at, and the structure it held still falls.</Caption>
</AppendixFigure>

This is impactful precisely because it reframes deepfakes as an *epistemological* problem, not just a fraud or privacy one: the threat isn't only the specific lies, it's the erosion of a background condition for trusting recordings at all. But — true to this appendix — the magnitude is contested, and the pushback is sharp and worth taking seriously. Joshua Habgood-Coote ("Deepfakes and the epistemic apocalypse," *Synthese*, 2023) argues the doom framing is overblown: we have never relied on recordings as infallible, we already cross-check testimony against many sources, and societies have absorbed media-manipulation panics before. Atencia-Linares and Artiga ("Deepfakes, shallow epistemic graves," *Synthese*, 2022) defend the residual epistemic robustness of photography and video. So the *mechanism* Rini names — recordings as a silent regulator of testimony — is a genuine and illuminating contribution; the *prediction* of an "epistemic apocalypse" or wholesale collapse of public knowledge is a live dispute, not a settled forecast. Bring this one forward to **Day 2**, where science's answer to "how do you trust a report you can't personally verify?" is a whole institutional machinery of replication and recording — and to the AI block, where it meets §3 head-on.

## Hostile epistemology: when the environment is built to fool you

<StatusChipRow><StatusChip status={"ok"} label={"Bubble≠chamber distinction · widely adopted"} printLabel={"established"} /><StatusChip status={"bad"} label={"scope & reality of \"echo chambers\" · contested"} printLabel={"contested"} /></StatusChipRow>
Traditional epistemology pictured a lone, neutral mind facing a neutral world. C. Thi Nguyen's program — he calls it <Term as="em">hostile epistemology</Term><TipNote text={"Hostile epistemology studies how information environments can be engineered to manipulate what people trust and believe."} /> — starts from a darker and more modern premise: your epistemic environment is not neutral. It is increasingly *engineered*, often by parties with an interest in what you come to believe, to exploit the predictable shortcuts your mind must use. Three of his post-2020 moves have reshaped how the whole field talks about online life.

The first is a distinction that sounds academic and turns out to be the key to everything: the difference between an <Term as="em">epistemic bubble</Term> and an <Term as="em">echo chamber</Term> ("Echo Chambers and Epistemic Bubbles," *Episteme*, 2020). They are *not* the same thing, and conflating them is why so many well-meaning fixes fail. In a bubble, outside voices are merely *absent* — you simply haven't been exposed to them (think a filter that only ever shows you agreeable sources). In a chamber, outside voices are *present but actively discredited* — you've been trained to *distrust* them in advance ("the mainstream media lies," "experts are corrupt"). The consequence is stark and counterintuitive.

The second move names a vulnerability inside your own head. In "The Seductions of Clarity" (*Royal Institute of Philosophy Supplement*, 2021), Nguyen argues that the *feeling* of clarity — that satisfying click when everything seems to fall into place — functions as a <Term as="em">thought-terminating heuristic</Term><TipNote text={"A heuristic is a quick rule of thumb: useful because it is fast, risky because it can mislead."} />. We use the sense that a matter has become clear as a signal that we've inquired enough and can stop. Usually fine. But it means clarity can be *weaponized*: a manipulator who can manufacture an exaggerated sense of clarity — a tidy ideology that explains everything, a conspiracy theory where every fact slots satisfyingly into place — can get you to *halt your inquiry early*, before you notice the holes. Notice how this snaps together with §1: clarity is dangerous precisely because it *terminates the zetetic process*. The slickest, most "it all makes sense now" account is, for that very reason, the one to interrogate hardest. The third move completes the toolkit: in "Trust as an Unquestioning Attitude" (*Oxford Studies in Epistemology*, 2022), Nguyen analyzes trust itself as the stance of *not questioning* — of taking something as a settled background you build on without re-checking. Indispensable (you can't re-derive everything from scratch), and exactly therefore exploitable: capture what someone trusts unquestioningly, and you've captured where they'll never think to look.

The calibrated tag here is twofold. The *conceptual* contributions — bubble vs. chamber, clarity as inquiry-terminating, trust as unquestioning — have been rapidly and widely adopted because they're genuinely clarifying and action-guiding. But two cautions earn their chips. First, philosophers are already pushing on the construct itself (Carey &amp; Ventham, "There is no fresh air: a problem with the concept of echo chambers," *Episteme*, 2025). Second — and this is a hype-filter point the course insists on — the *empirical* social-science picture of how prevalent real-world echo chambers actually are is genuinely mixed; several large studies find most people's media diets are more varied than the "sealed echo chamber" image suggests. So treat the *conceptual machinery* as a sharp and durable tool, and the *empirical scale* of the phenomenon as an open measurement question. The framework is the contribution; the size of the fire it describes is still being measured.

## Re-deriving Day 1's Dutch book — from truth, not money

<StatusChipRow><StatusChip status={"ok"} label={"Core accuracy-dominance result · established"} printLabel={"established"} /><StatusChip status={"bad"} label={"\"refounds all of rationality\" · contested"} printLabel={"contested"} /></StatusChipRow>
On Day 1, the Dutch book theorem justified probability laws through bets: if your credences violate those laws, a bookie can offer individually acceptable wagers that guarantee a collective loss. That is a pragmatic penalty. <Term as="em">Accuracy-first epistemology</Term>, also called epistemic utility theory<TipNote text={"Epistemic utility theory scores beliefs by their knowledge-related value, usually accuracy, the way decision theory scores actions by utility."} />, instead asks whether incoherent belief is defective because of its relation to truth.

The program, developed from James Joyce's 1998 "Nonpragmatic Vindication of Probabilism" through Richard Pettigrew's *Accuracy and the Laws of Credence* (2016) and later work, evaluates credences by *accuracy*: closeness to the truth. Under standard scoring assumptions, any incoherent set of credences — one that violates the probability laws — is <Term as="em">accuracy-dominated</Term> by a coherent set that is more accurate in every possible world. The objection to incoherence is then epistemic rather than financial: another set of credences is guaranteed to be closer to the truth.

The program tries to derive <Term as="em">probabilism</Term><TipNote text={"Probabilism is the view that rational degrees of belief should obey the probability axioms."} />, conditionalization, and other norms from the epistemic value of accuracy rather than from betting behavior or psychology. The core dominance theorem for probabilism is established mathematics. The broader claim that all epistemic norms follow from accuracy alone is contested: leading results depend on assumptions such as <Term as="em">additivity</Term><TipNote text={"Additivity says that when alternatives cannot both happen, the probability of either one is the sum of their separate probabilities."} /> and finite proposition sets, and rival accuracy measures can yield different verdicts (Chad Marxen, "Epistemic utility theory's difficult future," *Synthese*, 2021).

<SectionEyebrow>Open questions</SectionEyebrow>

## What the edge of the map leaves blank

- **Is inquiry the real unit?** Do the norms of seeking truly conflict with the norms of believing — and if so, which is fundamental?
- **Knowledge or belief first?** Is "factive theory of mind" the basic cognitive tool, with belief a later, costlier add-on — or is the knowledge/belief line itself too crisp?
- **What *do* machines produce?** Knowledge, assertion, testimony, instrument readings, or a new category of truth-apt text whose epistemic status remains disputed?
- **Friction or collapse?** Do deepfakes merely add cost to verifying recordings, or dissolve a load-bearing condition of public knowledge?
- **How engineered is your mind's environment** — and how big, really, are the echo chambers we can now so clearly *describe*?
- **Can truth alone ground rationality?** Does accuracy-first reach all the way, or only as far as its technical assumptions carry it?
- **And a quieter contender for a future day:** several of these point past knowledge toward *understanding* as the thing we actually prize — a pivot we'll feel again whenever a model can predict without explaining.
