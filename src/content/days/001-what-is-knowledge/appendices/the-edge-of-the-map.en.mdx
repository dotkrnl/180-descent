<section>
<p class="sec-eyebrow"><span class="n">§1</span> The activity turn</p>
<h2>Epistemology forgot to ask about <em>inquiry</em></h2>
<div class="chiprow"><StatusChip status={"hint"} label={"The puzzle is real · taken seriously"} printLabel={"promising"} /><StatusChip status={"bad"} label={"\"all norms are zetetic\" · contested"} printLabel={"contested"} /></div>
<p>Here is a strange omission, and once you see it you can't unsee it. For a century, epistemology has been almost entirely a theory of <em>states</em> — of belief, justification, knowledge: snapshots of a mind that has already finished thinking. It has had remarkably little to say about the <em>activity</em> that produces those states — the messy business of <em>inquiry</em>: asking a question, deciding what evidence to gather, knowing when to stop. Jane Friedman put a name to the missing half and lit a fuse under the field. The norms of inquiry she calls <em class="term">zetetic</em> norms (from the Greek <em>zētein</em>, to seek), and her landmark paper "The Epistemic and the Zetetic" (<em>Philosophical Review</em>, 2020) argues something genuinely destabilizing: the norms of <em>inquiry</em> and the norms of <em>belief</em> are not merely separate — they actively <strong>conflict</strong>.</p>
<p>The engine is a principle so obvious it sounds like a truism — the <em class="term">Zetetic Instrumental Principle</em>: if you want to figure out the answer to a question, you ought to take the necessary means to figure it out. Now watch it collide with a bedrock epistemic norm — the evidentialist's command that you may believe whatever your evidence already supports. Suppose you're trying to count the windows on the building across the street. Good inquiry says: <em>focus, go count the windows, don't get distracted.</em> But at every passing instant your senses hand you sufficient evidence to form, and be permitted to believe, a thousand idle truths — the color of that car, the number of people on the corner, the shape of a cloud. The belief-norm <em>permits</em> all of them. The inquiry-norm tells you to <em>ignore</em> all of them and count windows. Conform to one and you flout the other. The diagram makes the squeeze concrete.</p>

<p>Why does this matter beyond the seminar? Because it suggests epistemology has been studying the wrong unit. If belief-norms and inquiry-norms genuinely clash, then a theory built only on belief is incomplete — maybe even <em>backwards</em>. The radical proposal, the "zetetic turn," is that <strong>all epistemic norms are ultimately norms of inquiry</strong> (suspension of judgment becomes a <em>question-directed</em> attitude; believing an answer is a way of <em>closing</em> a question). The field has not swallowed this whole — and that's the honest part. Arianna Falbo ("Should epistemology take the zetetic turn?", <em>Philosophical Studies</em>, 2023) and others argue the inquiry-norms are really <em>practical</em>, not distinctively epistemic, and that a purely zetetic epistemology can't explain why some beliefs are irrational even when believing them would <em>help</em> your inquiry. So: the <em>puzzle</em> is now taken with great seriousness across the field; the <em>grand thesis</em> that inquiry swallows everything is a genuine, unresolved fight. Either way, the question "what is knowledge?" is quietly being reframed as "what is it to inquire <em>well</em>?" — and that reframing reaches all the way forward to <strong>Day 2</strong>, where the scientific method is exactly a set of norms for collective inquiry.</p>
</section>
<section>
<p class="sec-eyebrow"><span class="n">§2</span> The cognitive-science turn</p>
<h2>What if knowledge comes <em>before</em> belief?</h2>
<div class="chiprow"><StatusChip status={"hint"} label={"The empirical pattern · fairly robust"} printLabel={"promising"} /><StatusChip status={"bad"} label={"\"knowledge is more basic\" · contested"} printLabel={"contested"} /></div>
<p>Day 1 treated knowledge as something <em>built up from</em> belief: take a belief, add truth, add justification, screen out luck. Almost every theory we met assumed belief is the raw material and knowledge the finished product. A large interdisciplinary team led by Jonathan Phillips and Joshua Knobe dropped a target article in <em>Behavioral and Brain Sciences</em> — "Knowledge before belief" (2021) — arguing that, as a matter of how human (and animal) minds actually work, this may be exactly <strong>upside down</strong>.</p>
<p>The standard story in psychology is that our "theory of mind" — our capacity to model other minds — is centered on <em>belief</em>, and matures when a child finally passes the <em>false-belief test</em> (understanding that someone can hold a belief the child knows to be false) around age four. Phillips and colleagues marshal converging evidence that representing <em>knowledge</em> is the more basic feat. The threads: developmentally, infants and toddlers track who has <em>seen</em> or has <em>access to</em> information — who <em>knows</em> — well before they can handle false beliefs. Comparatively, non-human great apes show robust signs of tracking what others can perceive and know, while convincing evidence of belief-tracking remains elusive. And in adults, attributions of knowledge are made at least as fast as — sometimes faster than — attributions of belief, which is hard to explain if "X knows p" is computed by first building "X believes p" and then checking extra conditions. The picture they paint is a <em class="term">factive theory of mind</em>: the mind's first and most basic tool for modeling others is "what do they know?", with the trickier, error-tolerant "what do they merely <em>believe</em> (perhaps falsely)?" coming later and costing more.</p>

<p>If it holds, the payoff for Day 1 is direct and large. It would be empirical ammunition for Timothy Williamson's <em>knowledge-first</em> program — the philosophical claim (which we met as pure armchair theory) that knowledge is the basic, unanalyzable state and belief should be explained in terms of <em>it</em>, not the reverse. Suddenly that's not just a logician's hunch; it's a candidate fact about the architecture of cognition, with an evolutionary rationale (a social animal urgently needs to track <em>who has reliable information</em> — echoing Edward Craig's "good-informant" genealogy from Appendix I). But honesty demands the asterisk, and here it's loud: a BBS target article arrives wrapped in dozens of peer commentaries, and many dissent hard. Critics argue the knowledge/belief line is blurrier than the authors allow, that "tracking who saw what" needn't be a full representation of <em>knowledge</em>, and that culture and language shape the whole picture. So: the <em>behavioral findings</em> (kids and apes track informational access early) are reasonably solid; the <em>strong interpretation</em> (knowledge-representation is metaphysically and computationally prior, with belief assembled from it) is very much live. The furniture of the mind is being rearranged in real time, and the movers don't yet agree on the floor plan.</p>
</section>
<section>
<p class="sec-eyebrow"><span class="n">§3</span> The machine turn, part one</p>
<h2>Does a language model know anything — or just bullshit?</h2>
<div class="chiprow"><StatusChip status={"ok"} label={"Models are truth-indifferent · established"} printLabel={"established"} /><StatusChip status={"bad"} label={"\"bullshit\" as the right category · contested"} printLabel={"contested"} /></div>
<p>Day 1 ended on a needle of a question: when a system like the one that drafted these pages outputs a true, well-supported sentence, does it <em>know</em> anything — or is it the ultimate Gettier case, right for reasons that have nothing to do with the truth? The 2020s turned that closing flourish into one of the hottest debates in the field, and the most-discussed entry has a title that sailed past peer review unblunted: "ChatGPT is bullshit" (Hicks, Humphries &amp; Slater, <em>Ethics and Information Technology</em>, 2024).</p>
<p>Their move is precise, not merely rude. They borrow Harry Frankfurt's technical sense of <em class="term">bullshit</em> (from his 1986 essay <em>On Bullshit</em>): bullshit is speech produced with <em>indifference</em> to truth. The liar at least tracks the truth — he has to, in order to steer you away from it. The bullshitter doesn't care either way; he says whatever serves his purpose, and whether it's true is simply beside the point. Now consider what a large language model fundamentally <em>is</em>: a system trained to emit the statistically likely next <em class="term">token</em><TipNote text={"A token is the unit of text a language model predicts; it can be a whole word, a word piece, punctuation, or spacing."} />, to produce fluent, plausible-sounding text. It has no representation of truth that it is trying to honor. So when it states a real fact and when it "hallucinates" a fake citation, it is doing the <em>very same thing</em> — generating likely-looking text — and succeeding equally at its actual task in both cases. On this view, "hallucination" is a flattering misnomer that implies a malfunction; the truer description is that the system is <strong>indifferent to truth by design</strong>, which is bullshit in Frankfurt's exact sense. They distinguish <em>soft</em> bullshit (no intent to deceive, just truth-indifference) from <em>hard</em> bullshit (additionally posing as a sincere truth-teller), and argue an LLM is at minimum a soft bullshitter.</p>
<p>Why this could redraw the map: it cuts directly against the loose talk of machines "knowing," "understanding," or "believing." If the argument is right, an LLM's true outputs aren't knowledge and aren't even really <em>assertions</em> in the full sense — they're a new category of truth-apt-looking text with no one home who cares whether it's true. That reframes how we should trust, cite, and regulate these systems. And it is, predictably, <em>contested</em> — the rebuttals are already a small literature. Some argue the "bullshit" label smuggles in a stance on whether models have intentions at all (Sarah Fisher, "Large language models and their big bullshit potential," 2024; David Gunkel &amp; Simon Coghlan, "Cut the crap," 2025); others that as models are trained with reinforcement to be truthful and to express calibrated uncertainty, "indifferent to truth" is too crude. What's <em>settled</em> is the unglamorous core: a base language model has no built-in commitment to truth, and fluency is not knowledge. What's <em>open</em> is whether "bullshit," "instrument," "testifier," or some entirely new epistemic category is the right home for what these systems produce. It is the brain-in-a-vat from Appendix I made of silicon and shipped to a billion users — words that may never have touched the world, now answering our questions.</p>
</section>
<section>
<p class="sec-eyebrow"><span class="n">§4</span> The machine turn, part two</p>
<h2>The support beam you never noticed: the epistemic backstop</h2>
<div class="chiprow"><StatusChip status={"hint"} label={"The mechanism · plausible & influential"} printLabel={"promising"} /><StatusChip status={"bad"} label={"\"epistemic apocalypse\" · contested"} printLabel={"contested"} /></div>
<p>Appendix I established how much of what you know arrives by <em>testimony</em> — other people's say-so. Regina Rini's "Deepfakes and the Epistemic Backstop" (<em>Philosophers' Imprint</em>, 2020) identifies a hidden structural support that has quietly kept that whole edifice honest — and shows how a new technology is sawing through it.</p>
<p>The insight is subtle. Why is testimony as reliable as it is? Part of the answer, Rini argues, is a silent regulator: the ever-present <em>possibility</em> of a recording. When someone can be contradicted by a photograph, an audio clip, or a video, they have a standing incentive to testify truthfully — because a recording might surface and catch them out. Recordings function as an <em class="term">epistemic backstop</em>: not because we constantly check them, but because their mere availability disciplines testimony, the way an unused referee still shapes a game. For roughly a century — since photography and audio became hard to fake — we have built our norms of public truth-telling on this backstop without ever naming it. Deepfakes — convincingly fabricated video and audio, generated by the same machine-learning wave as §3 — dissolve it from both sides. They flood the channel with convincing fakes <em>and</em>, just as corrosively, they hand every caught wrongdoer a new escape: <em>that recording of me is probably a deepfake.</em> The "<em class="term">liar's dividend</em>"<TipNote text={"The liar’s dividend is the benefit liars get when real evidence can be dismissed as fake."} />. Once any recording can be waved away, the backstop stops disciplining testimony — and testimony itself, our single largest source of knowledge, loses a support we didn't know it leaned on. Don Fallis sharpens the same worry in information-theoretic terms ("The Epistemic Threat of Deepfakes," <em>Philosophy &amp; Technology</em>, 2021): deepfakes <em>reduce the information</em> a video carries about what actually happened, degrading it as a signal.</p>

<p>This is impactful precisely because it reframes deepfakes as an <em>epistemological</em> problem, not just a fraud or privacy one: the threat isn't only the specific lies, it's the erosion of a background condition for trusting recordings at all. But — true to this appendix — the magnitude is contested, and the pushback is sharp and worth taking seriously. Joshua Habgood-Coote ("Deepfakes and the epistemic apocalypse," <em>Synthese</em>, 2023) argues the doom framing is overblown: we have never relied on recordings as infallible, we already cross-check testimony against many sources, and societies have absorbed media-manipulation panics before. Atencia-Linares and Artiga ("Deepfakes, shallow epistemic graves," <em>Synthese</em>, 2022) defend the residual epistemic robustness of photography and video. So the <em>mechanism</em> Rini names — recordings as a silent regulator of testimony — is a genuine and illuminating contribution; the <em>prediction</em> of an "epistemic apocalypse" or wholesale collapse of public knowledge is a live dispute, not a settled forecast. Bring this one forward to <strong>Day 2</strong>, where science's answer to "how do you trust a report you can't personally verify?" is a whole institutional machinery of replication and recording — and to the AI block, where it meets §3 head-on.</p>
</section>
<section>
<p class="sec-eyebrow"><span class="n">§5</span> The adversarial turn</p>
<h2>Hostile epistemology: when the environment is built to fool you</h2>
<div class="chiprow"><StatusChip status={"ok"} label={"Bubble≠chamber distinction · widely adopted"} printLabel={"established"} /><StatusChip status={"bad"} label={"scope & reality of \"echo chambers\" · contested"} printLabel={"contested"} /></div>
<p>Traditional epistemology pictured a lone, neutral mind facing a neutral world. C. Thi Nguyen's program — he calls it <em class="term">hostile epistemology</em> — starts from a darker and more modern premise: your epistemic environment is not neutral. It is increasingly <em>engineered</em>, often by parties with an interest in what you come to believe, to exploit the predictable shortcuts your mind must use. Three of his post-2020 moves have reshaped how the whole field talks about online life.</p>
<p>The first is a distinction that sounds academic and turns out to be the key to everything: the difference between an <em class="term">epistemic bubble</em> and an <em class="term">echo chamber</em> ("Echo Chambers and Epistemic Bubbles," <em>Episteme</em>, 2020). They are <em>not</em> the same thing, and conflating them is why so many well-meaning fixes fail. In a bubble, outside voices are merely <em>absent</em> — you simply haven't been exposed to them (think a filter that only ever shows you agreeable sources). In a chamber, outside voices are <em>present but actively discredited</em> — you've been trained to <em>distrust</em> them in advance ("the mainstream media lies," "experts are corrupt"). The consequence is stark and counterintuitive.</p>
<p class="web-only">The interactive below lets you feel it: the obvious intervention — <em>expose people to the other side</em> — pops a bubble but can <em>strengthen</em> a chamber, because inside a chamber, encountering the enemy's argument is exactly what the chamber predicted, and so confirms it.</p>

<section>
<p>The second move names a vulnerability inside your own head. In "The Seductions of Clarity" (<em>Royal Institute of Philosophy Supplement</em>, 2021), Nguyen argues that the <em>feeling</em> of clarity — that satisfying click when everything seems to fall into place — functions as a <em class="term">thought-terminating heuristic</em><TipNote text={"A heuristic is a quick rule of thumb: useful because it is fast, risky because it can mislead."} />. We use the sense that a matter has become clear as a signal that we've inquired enough and can stop. Usually fine. But it means clarity can be <em>weaponized</em>: a manipulator who can manufacture an exaggerated sense of clarity — a tidy ideology that explains everything, a conspiracy theory where every fact slots satisfyingly into place — can get you to <em>halt your inquiry early</em>, before you notice the holes. Notice how this snaps together with §1: clarity is dangerous precisely because it <em>terminates the zetetic process</em>. The slickest, most "it all makes sense now" account is, for that very reason, the one to interrogate hardest. The third move completes the toolkit: in "Trust as an Unquestioning Attitude" (<em>Oxford Studies in Epistemology</em>, 2022), Nguyen analyzes trust itself as the stance of <em>not questioning</em> — of taking something as a settled background you build on without re-checking. Indispensable (you can't re-derive everything from scratch), and exactly therefore exploitable: capture what someone trusts unquestioningly, and you've captured where they'll never think to look.</p>
<p>The honest tag here is twofold. The <em>conceptual</em> contributions — bubble vs. chamber, clarity as inquiry-terminating, trust as unquestioning — have been rapidly and widely adopted because they're genuinely clarifying and action-guiding. But two cautions earn their chips. First, philosophers are already pushing on the construct itself (Carey &amp; Ventham, "There is no fresh air: a problem with the concept of echo chambers," <em>Episteme</em>, 2025). Second — and this is a hype-filter point the course insists on — the <em>empirical</em> social-science picture of how prevalent real-world echo chambers actually are is genuinely mixed; several large studies find most people's media diets are more varied than the "sealed echo chamber" image suggests. So treat the <em>conceptual machinery</em> as a sharp and durable tool, and the <em>empirical scale</em> of the phenomenon as an open measurement question. The framework is the contribution; the size of the fire it describes is still being measured.</p>
</section>
<section>
<p class="sec-eyebrow"><span class="n">§6</span> The formal refoundation</p>
<h2>Re-deriving Day 1's Dutch book — from truth, not money</h2>
<div class="chiprow"><StatusChip status={"ok"} label={"Core accuracy-dominance result · established"} printLabel={"established"} /><StatusChip status={"bad"} label={"\"refounds all of rationality\" · contested"} printLabel={"contested"} /></div>
<p>On Day 1 we justified the laws of probability with a <em>bribe</em>. The Dutch book theorem showed that if your credences break the probability rules, a clever bookie can sell you a set of bets you each accept as fair but which together guarantee you lose money. Powerful — but faintly unsatisfying as <em>epistemology</em>. Who cares about money? Shouldn't a <em>belief</em> be irrational for some reason to do with <em>truth</em>, not with your wallet? A research program that matured through the 2010s and is in full bloom now — <em class="term">accuracy-first epistemology</em>, also called epistemic utility theory<TipNote text={"Epistemic utility theory scores beliefs by their knowledge-related value, usually accuracy, the way decision theory scores actions by utility."} /> — answers exactly that, and it's one of the most elegant results in the modern subject.</p>
<p>The idea (seeded by James Joyce's 1998 "Nonpragmatic Vindication of Probabilism" and built out by Richard Pettigrew's <em>Accuracy and the Laws of Credence</em>, 2016, with a wave of 2020–2023 papers refining and contesting it) is to measure how good a set of credences is by a single epistemic yardstick: <em>accuracy</em>, its closeness to the truth. Full confidence in a truth is perfectly accurate; full confidence in a falsehood, maximally inaccurate. Now the theorem. For any <em>incoherent</em> credence — one that violates the probability laws — there is guaranteed to exist a <em>coherent</em> credence that is <strong>more accurate in every possible world at once</strong>. The incoherent one is, in the technical term, <em class="term">accuracy-dominated</em>: strictly beaten on truth-closeness no matter how things turn out. So you don't need the bookie at all. Incoherent confidence is irrational for a purely <em>epistemic</em> reason — it leaves guaranteed accuracy on the table; there's a better-aimed set of credences available that's closer to the truth whatever the world does.</p>
<p class="web-only">The interactive lets you see the domination geometrically.</p>

<section>
<p>What makes this a frontier rather than a footnote: it's an attempt to rebuild the <em>foundations</em> of rationality on a single epistemic value — getting close to the truth — and to derive not just <em class="term">probabilism</em><TipNote text={"Probabilism is the view that rational degrees of belief should obey the probability axioms."} /> but the update rule (conditionalization) and more besides from accuracy-dominance arguments. If it fully succeeds, the whole Bayesian edifice we started sketching on Day 1 rests on truth, not on betting behavior or psychology. The chip, though, is earned. The <em>core</em> dominance theorem for probabilism is established mathematics. The <em>ambition</em> — that all epistemic norms fall out of accuracy alone — is contested: the cleanest results lean on technical assumptions (<em class="term">additivity</em><TipNote text={"Additivity says that when alternatives cannot both happen, the probability of either one is the sum of their separate probabilities."} />, finitely many propositions) that critics argue smuggle in more than pure "closeness to truth" warrants (Chad Marxen, "Epistemic utility theory's difficult future," <em>Synthese</em>, 2021), and rival measures of accuracy can deliver different verdicts. So: a beautiful, genuinely illuminating reframing with a rock-solid center and a contested perimeter — which is, fittingly, the exact shape of this entire appendix.</p>
</section>
<div class="recap">
<p class="h">◆ The frontier in three sentences</p>
<dl>
<div><dt>Big idea</dt><dd>Since 2020 the question "what is knowledge?" has been pushed from five directions at once — reconceiving epistemology as the study of <em>inquiry</em> not belief (zetetic), reordering the mind so knowledge comes <em>before</em> belief, and confronting machines, deepfakes, and engineered information environments that strain or attack the very notion of a knower — while a formal program quietly rebuilds rationality's foundations on <em>truth</em> itself.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Best new analogy</dt><dd>The epistemic <em>backstop</em>: testimony has been kept honest all along by a support beam no one was looking at — the mere possibility of a recording — and deepfakes saw through it; pair it with the echo chamber, where the obvious fix (show them the other side) is precisely what makes the trap stronger.</dd></div>
<div><dt>Live controversy</dt><dd>Every item here is genuinely unsettled — whether inquiry-norms swallow belief-norms, whether knowledge-representation is really more basic than belief, whether "bullshit" is the right word for what LLMs do, whether deepfakes bring collapse or just friction, and whether accuracy alone can ground all of epistemic rationality — which is exactly why each wears a hype-filter tag.</dd></div>
</dl>

The five threads, now at the waterline.</p>

<p class="sec-eyebrow">Open questions</p>
<h2>What the edge of the map leaves blank</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Is inquiry the real unit?</strong> Do the norms of seeking truly conflict with the norms of believing — and if so, which is fundamental?</li>
<li><strong>Knowledge or belief first?</strong> Is "factive theory of mind" the basic cognitive tool, with belief a later, costlier add-on — or is the knowledge/belief line itself too crisp?</li>
<li><strong>What <em>do</em> machines produce?</strong> Knowledge, assertion, testimony, instrument-readings, or a genuinely new category of truth-apt text with no one home who cares?</li>
<li><strong>Friction or collapse?</strong> Do deepfakes merely add cost to verifying recordings, or dissolve a load-bearing condition of public knowledge?</li>
<li><strong>How engineered is your mind's environment</strong> — and how big, really, are the echo chambers we can now so clearly <em>describe</em>?</li>
<li><strong>Can truth alone ground rationality?</strong> Does accuracy-first reach all the way, or only as far as its technical assumptions carry it?</li>
<li><strong>And a quieter contender for a future day:</strong> several of these point past knowledge toward <em>understanding</em> as the thing we actually prize — a pivot we'll feel again whenever a model can predict without explaining.</li>
</ul>
