<p class="lede dropcap">In 2011 three psychologists set out to prove something impossible. They sat twenty undergraduates down, played half of them the Beatles' <em>"When I'm Sixty-Four"</em> and the other half a control tune, then asked a battery of questions — including each person's date of birth. After a perfectly ordinary statistical analysis, they announced their finding: listening to <em>"When I'm Sixty-Four"</em> made people <strong>a year and a half younger</strong>. Not feel younger. Be younger — their birth dates said so, at <MathInline latex={String.raw`p = .04`} />.</p>

<p>This is, of course, impossible. A song cannot reach back and edit the year you were born. And that was exactly the point. Joseph Simmons, Leif Nelson, and Uri Simonsohn had used only standard, respectable tools, made only choices that any working scientist makes every week — and produced a statistically significant impossibility. Their paper was a deliberately absurd demonstration designed to expose a vulnerability. Today we learn what it exposed, and how a science learns to stop fooling itself.</p>

<p class="sec-eyebrow">The hook</p>
<h2>The easiest person to fool</h2>
<blockquote>"The first principle is that you must not fool yourself — and you are the easiest person to fool." — Richard Feynman, Caltech commencement, 1974</blockquote>
<figure class="lesson-figure is-portrait">
<img src={feynman1959.src} width="899" height="900" alt="Richard Feynman standing at a blackboard in 1959." />
<figcaption>Richard Feynman at a blackboard in 1959. The quote above comes from his 1974 Caltech commencement address on "cargo cult science." <span class="meta">Image: <em>The Big T</em> 1959, public domain in the United States via Wikimedia Commons.</span></figcaption>
</figure>
<p>Feynman was talking about a kind of integrity that no formula can supply. The cruel twist of modern statistics is that the formulas can still be correct, but after flexible searching they no longer protect the nominal error rate. They lend the warm authority of mathematics to whatever you were already hoping to find.</p>
<p>The villain has a clinical name: <em class="term">researcher degrees of freedom</em><TipNote text={String.raw`Researcher degrees of freedom are the legitimate-looking analytic choices a researcher can make about data collection, cleaning, modeling, and reporting.`} />. Every real analysis hides dozens of small, defensible decisions. Which outliers to exclude? Should you control for age? Sex? Both? Do you stop collecting data now, or run twenty more participants? You measured three outcomes — which do you report? Each choice, taken alone, is innocent. Taken together, made <em>after</em> you've glanced at how the numbers are leaning, they become a machine for minting false discoveries.</p>
<p>Simmons, Nelson &amp; Simonsohn proved this with arithmetic before they proved it with the Beatles. By simulation, they showed that bundling just four ordinary degrees of freedom — peeking at the data and adding more if needed; choosing between two related outcome measures; splitting the sample by a variable like gender; dropping one of three experimental conditions — can inflate your chance of a false positive from the advertised <strong>5%</strong> to as high as <strong>61%</strong>. Flip enough innocent switches and a coin-flip's worth of noise becomes a near-certainty of "success."</p>

<p class="sec-eyebrow">The core model</p>
<h2>What a <MathInline latex={String.raw`p`} />-value actually says (and the six lies people tell about it)</h2>
<p>To not be fooled, you have to know precisely what the instrument reports. Many readers, including trained scientists and a worrying number of the statistics instructors teaching them, get this wrong. So, carefully:</p>

<div class="def">
<p class="h">Definition · the p-value</p>
      The <em class="term">p-value</em><TipNote text={String.raw`A p-value is a tail probability for data under a specified null model, not the probability that the null hypothesis is true.`} /> is the probability — <strong>computed on the assumption that the <em class="term">null model</em><TipNote text={String.raw`A null model is the world assumed by the p-value calculation, usually a model with no effect plus all stated sampling and modeling assumptions.`} />, the null hypothesis<TipNote text={String.raw`A null hypothesis is the baseline claim being tested, often that there is no effect or no difference.`} />, and every modeling assumption are true</strong> — of getting a result <strong>at least as extreme</strong> as the one you actually observed.
</div>

<p>Concrete version: suppose the null model says two groups have the same mean. If a difference this large or larger would occur 3% of the time under that model, then <MathInline latex={String.raw`p = .03`} />. That is <em>not</em> a 3% chance the null is true.</p>

<figure class="lesson-figure pvalue-figure">
<img src={pValueTailArea.src} data-light-src={pValueTailArea.src} data-dark-src={pValueTailAreaDark.src} width="800" height="460" loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="Course-styled diagram of a null-model bell curve with a shaded tail area labeled p-value, emphasizing that P(data given null) is not P(null given data)." />
<figcaption>The shaded tail is the p-value: the frequency of results this extreme or more extreme under the null model. <span class="meta">Adapted from Repapetilto and Chen-Pan Liao, CC BY-SA 3.0 via Wikimedia Commons.</span></figcaption>
</figure>

<p>Read it twice. It is a statement about <em>data, assuming a hypothesis</em> — written <MathInline latex={String.raw`\mathrm{P}(\text{data}\mid\text{null})`} />. It is emphatically <em>not</em> a statement about the hypothesis given the data, <MathInline latex={String.raw`\mathrm{P}(\text{null}\mid\text{data})`} />. Confusing those two is the exact inversion <strong>Day 4</strong> warned about with the disease-test problem: <MathInline latex={String.raw`\mathrm{P}(\text{positive}\mid\text{sick})`} /> is not <MathInline latex={String.raw`\mathrm{P}(\text{sick}\mid\text{positive})`} />, and no amount of staring turns one into the other without a prior. Here are the six misreadings to burn out of your reflexes — drawn from Greenland and colleagues' field guide to <em>twenty-five</em> of them:</p>

<div class="aside">
<p class="h">Three probabilities people confuse</p>
<p><strong><em class="term">Alpha</em></strong><TipNote text={String.raw`Alpha is the false-alarm rate you commit to before looking at the data, conditional on a true null model and a specified testing procedure.`} /> is the long-run Type I error rate of a testing procedure if the null model is true: with <MathInline latex={String.raw`\alpha=.05`} />, about 5% of true-null tests will falsely cross the line. The <strong><em class="term">false discovery rate</em></strong><TipNote text={String.raw`The false discovery rate is the fraction of declared discoveries that are false.`} /> asks a different question: among the claims you actually declare significant, what fraction are false? That depends on base rates, power, and selection, not just <MathInline latex={String.raw`\alpha`} />. A <strong><em class="term">posterior probability</em></strong><TipNote text={String.raw`A posterior probability is the probability of a hypothesis after combining data with prior information.`} />, such as <MathInline latex={String.raw`\mathrm{P}(\text{null}\mid\text{data})`} />, is different again: it needs a prior. A 5% <MathInline latex={String.raw`\alpha`} /> is neither a 5% false discovery rate nor a 5% chance the null is true.</p>
</div>

<ul class="mislist">
<li><b>"<MathInline latex={String.raw`p = .03`} /> means there's a 3% chance the null is true."</b><span class="fix">No. That's <MathInline latex={String.raw`\mathrm{P}(\text{null}\mid\text{data})`} /> — a Bayesian quantity needing a prior. The <MathInline latex={String.raw`p`} />-value already <em>assumes</em> the null.</span></li>
<li><b>"<MathInline latex={String.raw`p = .05`} /> means a 5% chance the result is just chance."</b><span class="fix">No. Chance (the null) is the very assumption the calculation is built on; it can't also be the thing in doubt.</span></li>
<li><b>"<MathInline latex={String.raw`p > .05`} /> means there is no effect."</b><span class="fix">No. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence — an underpowered study fails to detect real effects all the time.</span></li>
<li><b>"<MathInline latex={String.raw`1-p`} /> is the probability the alternative is true."</b><span class="fix">No. Neither <MathInline latex={String.raw`p`} /> nor its complement is a probability about any hypothesis.</span></li>
<li><b>"<em>p</em> tells you the result will replicate."</b><span class="fix">No. A low <MathInline latex={String.raw`p`} /> from one noisy study says little about the next study.</span></li>
<li><b>"A significant result is a large or important one."</b><span class="fix">No. With a big enough sample, a trivially small effect is "significant." Significance ≠ size ≠ importance.</span></li>
</ul>

<p>That last one is the quiet killer, so it gets its own model.</p>

<h3>Significance is not size: meet the effect size</h3>
<p>A <MathInline latex={String.raw`p`} />-value entangles effect size, noise, sample size, and model assumptions. Pour in enough participants and even a microscopic, meaningless difference crosses the line. So grown-up statistics insists on reporting the <em class="term">effect size</em><TipNote text={String.raw`An effect size reports how large a difference or association is, instead of only whether it crossed a significance threshold.`} /> separately: <em>Cohen's d</em><TipNote text={String.raw`Cohen’s d expresses a difference between two means in units of standard deviation.`} /> (a difference in means measured in standard deviations) or a correlation <em>r</em>. Jacob Cohen offered rough labels — <em>d</em> ≈ 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large — while cheerfully admitting they were arbitrary: "Although arbitrary, the proposed conventions will be found to be reasonable by reasonable people." The point is not the labels. The point is that a number with no effect size attached has told you almost nothing worth knowing.</p>

<h3>The interval you've been misreading too</h3>
<p><em class="term">Confidence intervals</em><TipNote text={String.raw`A confidence interval is a range produced by a method with a stated long-run coverage rate, such as 95%.`} /> are sold as the humane alternative to <MathInline latex={String.raw`p`} />-values, and they are better — but they hide their own trap.</p>
<div class="def">
<p class="h">Definition · the 95% confidence interval</p>
      A 95% CI is produced by a <strong>procedure</strong> that, across many hypothetical repeat experiments, brackets the true value 95% of the time. The "95%" describes the long-run reliability of the <em>method</em>, not the odds for your one particular interval.
</div>
<p>So the natural sentence — "there's a 95% chance the true value is in <em>this</em> interval" — is, in the frequentist world, simply false. Your interval either contains the truth or it doesn't; the dice were thrown when you ran the study. (A <strong>Day 4</strong> Bayesian <em>credible</em> interval <em>does</em> license that sentence — but only after you've committed to a prior. Different tool, different promise.) Nor does a CI mean 95% of your data live inside it, nor that values just outside are impossible. What it gives you, refreshingly, is a <em>range of effect sizes compatible with your data</em> — and that reframing, from "yes/no" to "how much, how precisely," is the heart of what reformers call the <em>new statistics</em>.</p>

<h3>Why small studies can exaggerate what they detect</h3>
<p>Two ways to be wrong: a <em class="term">Type I error</em><TipNote text={String.raw`A Type I error is a false positive: rejecting a true null hypothesis.`} /> (false alarm — declaring an effect that isn't there) and a <em class="term">Type II error</em><TipNote text={String.raw`A Type II error is a false negative: missing a real effect.`} /> (a miss — failing to see one that is). <em class="term">Statistical power</em><TipNote text={String.raw`Statistical power is the probability that a study will detect an effect of a given size if that effect is real.`} /> is your chance of catching a real effect; more data buys more power. You'd assume an underpowered study just yields shrugs and "no result." Worse: it actively poisons the literature. When power is low, the only estimates lucky enough to clear the significance bar are the substantially inflated ones — the <em class="term">winner's curse</em><TipNote text={String.raw`The winner’s curse is the tendency for selected significant estimates from noisy studies to exaggerate the true effect.`} />. Pair that with publication practices that preferentially reward significant findings, and you manufacture a published record of effects that are both exaggerated and fragile. A sobering 2013 audit ("Power failure," Button et al.) put the median power across neuroscience at roughly <strong>21%</strong>; a 2017 reanalysis by Nord and colleagues usefully qualifies the headline, arguing that power is not uniformly low across every neuroscience subfield. The lesson survives the caveat: where studies are underpowered, real effects are missed and the effects that surface are often inflated.</p>
</section>

<section>
<p class="sec-eyebrow">The deeper trap</p>
<h2>You don't have to cheat to be fooled</h2>
<p>Here is the part that should keep honest people up at night. You might read all of the above, swear off p-hacking, run exactly <strong>one</strong> pre-planned analysis, and report it faithfully — and <em>still</em> have an inflated false-positive rate. This is Andrew Gelman and Eric Loken's <em class="term">garden of forking paths</em><TipNote text={String.raw`The garden of forking paths is the set of analyses a researcher might reasonably have chosen after seeing different patterns in the same data.`} />.</p>
<p>Their insight is subtle and a little haunting. The damage doesn't require running many analyses. It only requires that the single analysis you ran was <em>contingent on the data you happened to see</em>. Suppose your data had come out slightly differently — a touch noisier here, a cluster there. You'd have made a different, equally reasonable choice: controlled for age instead of income, compared women instead of the whole sample, used a median instead of a mean. The other analyses are invisible because you never ran them. But the universe of paths you <em>would have</em> taken, in nearby worlds, is exactly the multiplicity that inflates your error rate. As Gelman and Loken put it, you can get a false positive with no "fishing expedition" and no p-hacking at all, even with the hypothesis fixed in advance.</p>
<p>Notice how this rhymes with <a href="/days/005-causation/"><strong>Day 5</strong></a>. There, the lurking decision was <em>which variables to condition on</em> — and conditioning on a <em class="term">collider</em><TipNote text={String.raw`A collider is a variable influenced by two other variables; conditioning on it can create a misleading association between its causes.`} /> could <em>manufacture</em> a correlation out of nothing. The forking paths are the same hazard generalized: every defensible analytic choice is a junction, and the data quietly nudge you down the branch that flatters your hypothesis. The honest analyst and the p-hacker can produce the identical false result. The difference is only that one of them knows they did it.</p>

<div class="aside">
<p class="h">The fix that names the disease</p>
<p>If the problem is that choices get made <em>after</em> seeing the data, the cleanest cure is to make them <em>before</em>: <em class="term">preregistration</em><TipNote text={String.raw`Preregistration means recording hypotheses, data handling, and analysis plans before looking at the results.`} /> (write down your analysis plan, publicly, in advance) and <em class="term">Registered Reports</em><TipNote text={String.raw`Registered Reports are journal articles accepted in principle after peer review of the research question and method, before the results are known.`} /> (journals peer-review and accept the <em>plan</em>, before any results exist). We met these on <a href="/days/002-scientific-method-and-demarcation/"><strong>Day 2</strong></a>; here's <em>why</em> they bite. They freeze the confirmatory path; deviations become exploratory rather than hidden, converting a garden of "reasonable choices I made while peeking" into a single committed road.</p>
</div>
</section>

</section>

<section>
<p>What unites the whole spectrum, from cautious to incendiary, is a single migration: away from a yes/no verdict at a magic number, toward <em>reporting how big an effect is, how uncertain you are, and how fragile the answer is to the choices you made</em>. That last idea — fragility — is where the live 2020s frontier lives.</p>
</section>

<section class="frontier">
<p class="sec-eyebrow">The frontier · 2024–2026</p>
<h2>Making fragility visible — and the hype filter</h2>
<p>If a single analysis can mislead, the modern move is brutally simple: <em>run them all</em>, and show the spread. Two named methods do this, and a third line of work tests the whole idea by turning loose armies of real scientists on the same data. As always, each claim gets a label.</p>

<div class="ctop">
<span class="cnum">Edge 01</span>

<h3>Multiverse &amp; specification-curve analysis</h3>
<p>Instead of defending one analysis, you enumerate <em>every</em> reasonable combination of choices — keep outliers or trim them, log-transform or not, control for this covariate or that — and compute the result under each. <em class="term">Multiverse analysis</em><TipNote text={String.raw`Multiverse analysis runs many defensible analysis choices and reports the distribution of results rather than one preferred path.`} /> (Steegen, Tuerlinckx, Gelman &amp; Vanpaemel, <em>Perspectives on Psychological Science</em>, 2016) displays the whole cloud of outcomes. <em class="term">Specification-curve analysis</em><TipNote text={String.raw`Specification-curve analysis sorts many defensible model specifications by their estimated effect to reveal robustness or fragility.`} /> (Simonsohn, Simmons &amp; Nelson, <em>Nature Human Behaviour</em>, 2020) sorts hundreds of specifications into a single curve and asks: across all the defensible ways to slice this, does the effect hold up, or does it evaporate the moment you wiggle a choice? It's honest, vivid, and increasingly expected by reviewers. Try the curve now, before the caveats continue.</p>
<p>One discipline matters more than the picture suggests: some "reasonable specifications" change the <em class="term">estimand</em><TipNote text={String.raw`An estimand is the exact quantity a study is trying to estimate, such as a raw association, a causal effect, or an effect for a specific population.`} />. Controlling for W is not just another estimate of the same question unless W is a causally justified control; rank-transforming, trimming outliers, or dropping a subgroup can shift the target from a mean difference to an ordinal association, a robust effect, or a population-specific effect. Specification curves expose that fork, but they do not decide which question you meant to ask.</p>
<p>The <strong>hype filter</strong> matters here, though. These are superb <em>transparency</em> devices and weak <em>inferential</em> ones. The authors of specification-curve analysis concede the catch themselves: deciding which specifications are "reasonable" is a judgment call no algorithm can make, and "the goal to eliminate subjectivity is unattainable." A determined arguer can still curate the multiverse. And there is no settled way to compute a single valid conclusion from a curve — which is why 2024 brought new machinery (PIMA, below) trying to supply one. <StatusChip status={"ok"} label={"established as a robustness display"} printLabel={"established"} /> &nbsp;<StatusChip status={"bad"} label={"oversold if treated as an objectivity machine"} printLabel={"review"} /></p>

<h3>Many analysts, one dataset — the clearest demonstration in the room</h3>
<p>Here is the experiment that should change how you read every headline. Take one dataset, one clear question, and hand identical copies to dozens of expert teams. Do they converge on the same verdict? Often, no.</p>
<p>In Silberzahn et al. (<em>Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science</em>, 2018), <strong>29 teams</strong> (61 analysts) were asked a single question — do soccer referees give more red cards to dark-skin-toned players? Their estimated odds ratios ranged from <strong>0.89 to 2.93</strong>; twenty teams found a significant positive effect, nine did not. Tellingly, the analysts' prior beliefs and even their statistical expertise did <em>not</em> explain who found what. In Botvinik-Nezer et al. (<em>Nature</em>, 2020), <strong>70 teams</strong> (180 researchers) analyzed the same brain-imaging dataset against nine pre-set hypotheses — and no two teams used the same pipeline; their yes/no conclusions diverged sharply even when their underlying statistical maps were highly correlated. Breznau et al. (<em>PNAS</em>, 2022) put the same data and hypothesis (does immigration erode support for social policy?) to <strong>73 teams</strong> and watched estimates scatter from clearly negative to clearly positive. Even finance has its version: Menkveld et al.'s "Nonstandard Errors" (<em>Journal of Finance</em>, 2024) had <strong>164 teams</strong> test the same hypotheses on the same market data, and found the cross-team variability rivaled ordinary statistical error — shrinking, encouragingly, when extra peer-review stages were added.</p>
<p>This is <a href="/days/001-what-is-knowledge/"><strong>Day 1</strong></a>'s Gettier problem made flesh, inside a single dataset. Each team's belief is "justified" by a competent analysis; whether it's <em>true</em> — connected to reality rather than to their particular path through the garden — varies team to team. One analysis is one branch. Treat it accordingly.</p>

<h3>Plugging the inference gap — and Day 4's quiet alternative</h3>
<p>The open problem in all of the above is honest <em>inference</em> across a multiverse: how do you draw one valid conclusion from a thousand entangled analyses? A 2024 entry, <em class="term">PIMA</em> (Post-selection Inference in Multiverse Analysis; Girardi et al., <em>Psychometrika</em>, 2024), offers a sign-flipping test that aims to give the multiverse a proper joint error guarantee, reaching beyond the linear-model limits of the 2020 specification curve. It's peer-reviewed and genuinely interesting, but new and not yet standard practice — a <strong>promising hint</strong>, not a settled tool.</p>
<p>Meanwhile the boring, durable fixes keep spreading: the Registered Reports format — where journals accept your <em>plan</em> before your results exist — is now offered at <strong>several hundred journals</strong>, and preregistration is becoming a default rather than a virtue. And recall <a href="/days/004-probability-as-extended-logic/"><strong>Day 4</strong></a>'s <em class="term">e-values</em><TipNote text={String.raw`E-values measure evidence as the payoff of a betting strategy against a null hypothesis.`} /> and "testing by betting" (Grünwald, Ramdas, Shafer): an alternative to the <MathInline latex={String.raw`p`} />-value that stays valid even if you peek at your data and stop whenever you like — aimed squarely at the optional-stopping degree of freedom you toggled in the factory above. Still a research frontier rather than mainstream practice — <strong>promising</strong>, watch this space.</p>

<section>
<p class="sec-eyebrow">Open questions</p>
<h2>What's genuinely unsettled</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Should "statistical significance" survive at all?</strong> Reformers are split between disciplining the threshold and abolishing it. Decisions still have to be made somewhere — a drug is approved or it isn't — and abolitionists owe an account of how.</li>
<li><strong>Can a multiverse ever yield a single honest verdict?</strong> Or is the choice of "reasonable specifications" an irreducibly subjective act that just relocates the forking paths one level up? PIMA and friends are early attempts; the jury is out.</li>
<li><strong>Is variation in <em>conclusions</em> the same as variation in <em>reality</em>?</strong> A subtle 2023 reanalysis noted that in some many-analyst studies the headline-grabbing disagreement was about <em>significance</em>, while the underlying effect sizes were quietly consistent and small. Disagreement about a verdict can exceed disagreement about the number. Don't over-learn "everything is hopeless."</li>
<li><strong>Do <MathInline latex={String.raw`p`} />-values or e-values deserve the future?</strong> The betting-based tools elegantly solve optional stopping but can demand more data and import a new modeling burden (which bet?). Coexistence looks likelier than conquest.</li>
<li><strong>And the question waiting in the AI block:</strong> when a model trained on millions of papers reports a "robust" result, has it learned to reason about evidence — or to imitate the very forking-path habits that got us here? (<strong>Days 138–145</strong>.)</li>
</ul>
