## Hume kicks out the leg

Start where the trouble starts. In 1739, in *A Treatise of Human Nature*, David Hume asked a deceptively simple question: when one billiard ball strikes another and the second rolls away, *what exactly do you see?* You see the first ball move. You see them touch. You see the second ball move. What you never see — look as hard as you like — is the **causing** itself: the necessary connection, the hidden force, the little arrow of *because* linking the two events.

All we ever actually observe, Hume argued, is <Term as="em">constant conjunction</Term><TipNote text={String.raw`Constant conjunction means one kind of event has repeatedly been followed by another kind of event.`} />: events of this type are reliably followed by events of that type. Add that the cause comes first (priority) and that the two events touch in space and time (contiguity), and you have everything experience delivers. On a standard Humean reading, the sense of *necessity* — the feeling that the second ball *had* to move — comes from customary expectation rather than from perceiving a necessary connection. Hume found two definitions of "cause" tangled together in our heads: one about the world (constant conjunction) and one about us (the mind's practiced leap from one to the other).

This should feel familiar. It is closely related to the **[problem of induction from Day 2](/days/002-scientific-method-and-demarcation/)**, wearing a different coat. If causation is just "this has always been followed by that," then claiming the next collision will behave like the last is precisely the unprovable bet on the uniformity of nature that Hume argued can never be justified non-circularly. The challenge generated competing regularity, counterfactual, probabilistic, and interventionist accounts; no single account is generally accepted as resolving every major class of difficult case.

<Aside>
<BlockTitle>Why "the cement of the universe"?</BlockTitle>

The phrase gets attached to Hume, but it is better treated as Mackie's title and image: **J. L. Mackie's 1974 book** is called *The Cement of the Universe*. This "cement" is the one thing we can never see. We infer the glue only from the fact that the bricks keep ending up stuck together.

## Saying what "more" there is

If causation is *more* than constant conjunction, the obvious move is to say what the "more" is. The twentieth century produced several serious answers — different ways to finish the sentence "C causes E means...". They are not simple rivals so much as lenses that modern causal modeling keeps borrowing from.

<div class="theory-key">
<div class="k"><span class="who2">Lewis · 1973</span><b><em class="term">Counterfactual</em><TipNote text={String.raw`A counterfactual asks what would have happened if some fact had been different.`} />.</b> In simple cases, E counterfactually depends on C: <em>had C not happened, E would not have happened.</em> Lewis then analyzes causation more generally through chains of such causal dependence. Clean and intuitive, but it has to wrestle with backup causes and double-killings (preemption and overdetermination).</div>
<div class="k"><span class="who2">Reichenbach · Suppes · Cartwright</span><b>Probabilistic.</b> These are different probabilistic programs, but the shared slogan is that causes <em>raise the probability</em> of their effects. Reichenbach's <em class="term">common-cause principle</em><TipNote text={String.raw`The common-cause principle says a surprising correlation often needs either a direct cause or a shared prior cause that explains it.`} />: if A and B are correlated but neither causes the other, a shared cause C "screens them off" — hold C fixed and the correlation dissolves in the model. Summer is the intended common-cause explanation in the toy example.</div>
<div class="k"><span class="who2">Woodward · 2003</span><b>Interventionist.</b> <MathInline latex={String.raw`C`} /> is a cause of <MathInline latex={String.raw`E`} /> when an appropriate <em class="term">intervention</em><TipNote text={String.raw`An intervention is an idealized change that sets one variable while blocking the usual causes of that variable.`} /> changes <MathInline latex={String.raw`C`} /> while breaking <MathInline latex={String.raw`C`} />'s usual causes, avoiding independent routes to <MathInline latex={String.raw`E`} />, and thereby changes <MathInline latex={String.raw`E`} />. No human needed: volcanoes cause ash even with no one to push the button. This is closely aligned with the interventionist semantics of structural causal models: below, Pearl turns the idea into <MathInline latex={String.raw`\operatorname{do}(C)`} /> and graph surgery.</div>
<div class="k"><span class="who2">Mackie · 1974</span><b>INUS conditions.</b> A cause can be an insufficient but non-redundant part of an unnecessary but sufficient condition. A short circuit is not enough by itself to burn a house down; it matters because it is a non-redundant part of one sufficient package: short circuit plus oxygen plus flammable material plus no timely suppression.</div>
</div>

<p class="theory-note">One question, several lenses: counterfactual = "what if it hadn't?"; probabilistic = "does it raise the odds, holding rivals fixed?"; INUS = "what role did this part play in a sufficient package?"; interventionist = "what changes under the right kind of intervention?". Structural causal models provide a precise language for many counterfactual and interventionist questions, though they do not settle every philosophical account of causation. The statistical frameworks that follow ask a narrower operational question: once an intervention and target effect have been specified, what assumptions let data identify that effect? Notice how <strong>Cartwright (1979)</strong> sharpened the probabilistic story, because her fix is the hinge of the whole day. Causes raise the probability of effects, yes — <em>but only inside a "causally homogeneous" background</em>, with the relevant other causes held fixed. Forget that proviso and you walk straight into the most beautiful trap in statistics.</p>
</ContentSection>

## Simpson's paradox: when the numbers literally reverse

Here is a fact that sounds impossible until you've seen it: a treatment can have a higher observed success rate among small stones, a higher observed success rate among large stones, and yet a lower observed success rate overall. The real kidney-stone dataset below lets you watch the bars flip.

A Simpson reversal occurs because the groups being combined have different mixtures of subgroups. The reversal itself does *not* tell you which comparison is causally relevant. That depends on what the third variable is: a <Term as="em">confounder</Term><TipNote text={String.raw`A confounder is a third variable that influences both the apparent cause and the apparent effect, making them move together.`} /> that should be adjusted for; a mediator, where adjustment changes a total-effect question into a direct-effect question; a collider or selection variable, where adjustment can create bias; or merely a descriptive partition involved in algebraic or non-collapsibility behavior. Causal knowledge, not the reversal alone, decides whether pooling or stratifying answers the right question.

In the kidney-stone data (Charig et al., *BMJ*, 1986), this was a historical, nonrandomized comparison of open surgery **A** and percutaneous nephrolithotomy **B**. The B cases contained a much larger share of small stones. B therefore has the higher pooled success rate, while A has the higher observed success rate within each reported stone-size stratum. Those observational strata demonstrate unequal case mix and a reversal; by themselves, they do not establish that open surgery would have produced better outcomes for the same patients.

The Reversal Machine below turns that into one picture. Each square is one patient and never moves. Color says which treatment they received; row says whether the case was small-stone or large-stone; fill says whether the treatment worked. The pooled view compares the two treatment columns. The stratified view keeps the same picture but compares inside each row first. In this example, stone size is a plausible baseline severity variable, so stratification is informative; in other graphs the same move could answer a different causal question or create bias.

## Pearl's ladder, and the verb that changed everything

Judea Pearl won the 2011 Turing Award "for fundamental contributions to artificial intelligence through the development of a calculus for probabilistic and causal reasoning." His central image, popularized in *The Book of Why* (2018), is the influential <Term as="em">Ladder of Causation</Term><TipNote text={String.raw`Pearl’s ladder separates association, intervention, and counterfactual reasoning into increasingly demanding levels.`} />: association, intervention, and counterfactuals. It is a powerful conceptual hierarchy, not an uncontested taxonomy every causal-inference tradition states in exactly the same form.

<ContentSection>
### The do-operator: seeing is not doing

Here is the conceptual hinge of the entire modern field, and it is worth saying slowly. There are two very different things you can do with a variable X.

You can **condition**<TipNote text={String.raw`Conditioning means narrowing attention to cases where some variable already has a given value.`} /> on it — written <MathInline latex={String.raw`P(Y \mid X = x)`} />. This means: among all the cases where X *happened* to equal x, what's the distribution of Y? You're filtering an existing population. This is *seeing*.

Or you can **intervene** — written, in Pearl's notation, <MathInline latex={String.raw`P(Y \mid \operatorname{do}(X = x))`} />. This means: consider a regime that sets X to x in the target population, snipping X away from whatever usually causes it, and then watch Y. This is *doing*. In an ideal randomized trial, assignment is designed to approximate that severing operation.

The two may coincide, but equality requires a design or causal assumptions; it does not follow from their numerical similarity. <MathInline latex={String.raw`P(Y \mid X=x)`} /> describes outcomes among cases in which X was observed to equal x. <MathInline latex={String.raw`P(Y \mid \operatorname{do}(X=x))`} /> describes outcomes in a hypothetical regime that sets X to x. The machine below makes the difference auditable by calculating both quantities from an explicit structural model.

</ContentSection>

<Aside>
<BlockTitle>The potential-outcomes lens</BlockTitle>

The other major language of modern causal inference writes the same idea at the level of units. For unit <MathInline latex={String.raw`i`} />, <Term as="em">potential outcomes</Term><TipNote text={String.raw`Potential outcomes are the outcomes a unit would have under different treatment or intervention values, even though only one is observed.`} /> are <MathInline latex={String.raw`Y_i(1)`} /> and <MathInline latex={String.raw`Y_i(0)`} />: the outcome if treated and the outcome if untreated. A common <Term as="em">causal estimand</Term><TipNote text={String.raw`An estimand is the precise causal quantity a study is trying to learn, such as an average treatment effect.`} /> is the average treatment effect in a specified target population and time horizon, <MathInline latex={String.raw`ATE = E[Y(1)-Y(0)]`} />. The catch is the fundamental problem of causal inference: for any one unit, only one of those two potential outcomes is observed. The other is counterfactual.

Designs and assumptions connect this target to data. <Term as="em">Consistency</Term><TipNote text={String.raw`Consistency says a unit observed under treatment value x has the same outcome as its potential outcome under x.`} /> links observed outcomes to potential outcomes under the treatment actually received. <Term as="em">Exchangeability</Term><TipNote text={String.raw`Exchangeability says treated and untreated groups are comparable with respect to the relevant potential outcomes, possibly after conditioning on covariates.`} /> says the groups being compared are comparable with respect to the relevant potential outcomes. <Term as="em">Positivity</Term><TipNote text={String.raw`Positivity, or overlap, means each relevant covariate group has a nonzero chance of receiving each treatment level being compared.`} /> means every relevant covariate group has a chance of receiving each treatment level. <Term as="em">No interference</Term><TipNote text={String.raw`No interference means one unit’s treatment does not change another unit’s outcome, unless such spillovers are explicitly modeled.`} /> means one unit's treatment does not alter another unit's outcome, unless spillovers are part of the model.

A safe causal workflow is: specify the unit, exposure, outcome, timing, and intervention; define the estimand; state the causal assumptions; determine whether the estimand is identified; estimate it statistically; then assess uncertainty, measurement, attrition, selection, overlap, and sensitivity to violations.

</Aside>

<ContentSection>
That severed arrow in the right-hand graph is the do-operator made visual. Intervening doesn't just *look* at X — it **deletes the arrows pointing into X** and replaces them with your hand. The rain→umbrella link is cut, so the observational path umbrella ← rain → wetness is no longer open in the umbrella contrast. Under this graph, the back-door-adjusted contrast recovers the intervention contrast.

Pearl gave us a grammar for doing this on paper. The recursive or acyclic <Term as="em">structural causal models</Term><TipNote text={String.raw`A structural causal model represents how variables are generated from other variables and from background noise.`} /> used in this lesson are represented by <Term as="em">directed acyclic graphs</Term><TipNote text={String.raw`A directed acyclic graph, or DAG, is a network of arrows with no directed loops.`} />: boxes and arrows with no directed loops. Cyclic and dynamic structural models also exist. In a DAG, a <Term as="em">fork</Term> (X ← Z → Y) can be a confounder; a <Term as="em">chain</Term> (X → Z → Y) can be a mediator; and a <Term as="em">collider</Term> (X → Z ← Y) is the trap. A path that enters a node through two arrowheads is normally closed at that collider. Conditioning on the collider — or sometimes one of its descendants — can open that path and induce an association along that route. This is why adjusting for every measured variable can introduce serious bias.

<Aside>
<BlockTitle>The front-door trick</BlockTitle>

Pearl's <Term as="em">front-door criterion</Term><TipNote text={String.raw`The front-door criterion identifies a causal effect through a measured mediator when the direct cause-effect path is confounded and specific graphical conditions hold.`} /> is one of the framework's neatest moves: sometimes you can identify a total effect even when the exposure-outcome path is confounded, by using a measured mediator. The usual textbook smoking → tar → cancer story is a stylized graph, not a settled description of the biological system. The standard criterion requires three graphical conditions: the mediator intercepts all directed paths from treatment to outcome; there is no unblocked back-door path from treatment to mediator; and treatment blocks all back-door paths from mediator to outcome. You still also need well-defined interventions, consistency, adequate support, and the usual measurement and selection cautions.

When those conditions hold, the calculation has three moves: estimate how treatment changes the mediator; estimate how the mediator changes the outcome while accounting for treatment; then average those pieces over the observed treatment mix.

<Formula>
<MathBlock latex={String.raw`P(y \mid \operatorname{do}(x)) = \sum_m P(m \mid x) \sum_{x'} P(y \mid m, x') P(x')`} />
</Formula>

It identifies an interventional distribution from observational data under the assumed graph — but only because you supplied a graph strong enough to license the expression.

</Aside>

## Three live edges — and the hype filter

Now the question that has launched a thousand papers and at least one industry: **can you infer causation from observation alone?** The honest answer is a precise "partly — and there's a proven wall." Each claim below is tagged for how much weight it can bear. <Meta>Evidence represented through 2024; page editorially reviewed June 2026.</Meta>

<div class="claim">
<div class="ctop">
<span class="cnum">Edge 01</span>
<StatusChip status={"ok"} label={"do-calculus completeness · proven theorem"} printLabel={"established"} />
<StatusChip status={"ok"} label={"Markov-equivalence ceiling · proven"} printLabel={"established"} />
</div>
<h3>The two theorems that fence the field</h3>
<p>This is the most solid ground in the whole day — not empirical findings that could be overturned, but <em>mathematical proofs</em>. First, <strong>do-calculus is complete</strong> for a scoped problem: given an assumed nonparametric causal graph and the available observational distribution, it is complete for identifying the specified intervention distribution. If identification fails in that problem, no alternative manipulation of the same observational information can identify it. Additional experiments, longitudinal information, parametric restrictions, valid proxies, or stronger functional assumptions change the information available and can change the answer.</p>
<Aside>
<BlockTitle>What a do-calculus rewrite looks like</BlockTitle>

For a simple back-door graph, where Z blocks every back-door path from X to Y and no element of Z is caused by X:

<Formula>
<MathBlock latex={String.raw`P(y \mid \operatorname{do}(x)) = \sum_z P(y \mid \operatorname{do}(x), z) P(z \mid \operatorname{do}(x)) = \sum_z P(y \mid x, z) P(z)`} />
</Formula>

The first equality is ordinary total probability over Z. The second uses the graph twice: once to replace the intervention on X with observation of X after conditioning on the sufficient adjustment set Z, and once to say intervening on X does not change the pre-treatment distribution of Z. The graph licenses exactly which symbols may be erased or exchanged.

</Aside>
<p>Second, the wall on the other side: <strong>the Markov-equivalence ceiling.</strong> Under causal Markov, faithfulness, and a specified treatment of latent variables and selection, conditional-independence patterns can rule out some graphs but not all. X→Y→Z, X←Y←Z, and X←Y→Z share a skeleton and have no differing unshielded colliders, so they imply the same conditional-independence pattern: X and Z are independent once you know Y. They form a <em class="term">Markov equivalence class</em><TipNote text={String.raw`A Markov equivalence class is a set of causal graphs that imply the same conditional independences, so observational data of that kind cannot choose among them.`} />. The collider X→Y←Z differs because conditioning on Y can open the path through Y. The takeaway is stark but scoped: observational independence information alone cannot deliver a unique causal graph without assumptions — only a class of candidates. Both results are <StatusChip status={"ok"} label={"ESTABLISHED"} printLabel={"established"} /> in the strongest sense available: theorems.</p>
</div>

<div class="claim">
<div class="ctop">
<span class="cnum">Edge 02</span>
<StatusChip status={"hint"} label={"direction-from-data · works under assumptions"} printLabel={"promising"} />
<StatusChip status={"ok"} label={"RCTs &amp; the credibility revolution · established"} printLabel={"established"} />
</div>
<h3>Squeezing direction out of still data — and why randomized assignment is still the benchmark</h3>
<p>So is the ceiling the end? Not quite — you can climb over it by importing extra assumptions the bare independence-tests don't use. <strong><em class="term">LiNGAM</em></strong><TipNote text={String.raw`LiNGAM is a causal-discovery method whose name abbreviates linear non-Gaussian acyclic model.`} /> (Shimizu et al., <em>JMLR</em>, 2006) showed that if relationships are linear, acyclic, have no hidden confounders, have mutually independent disturbances, and the disturbances are <em>non-Gaussian</em>, the full direction becomes identifiable — the asymmetry of non-Gaussian noise breaks the X→Y / Y→X tie. <strong><em class="term">Additive-noise models</em></strong><TipNote text={String.raw`Additive-noise models assume the effect equals a function of the cause plus independent noise, an asymmetry that can sometimes identify direction.`} /> (Hoyer, Janzing, Mooij, Peters &amp; Schölkopf, NIPS 2008 proceedings, published 2009) extended this to nonlinear cause-effect pairs. On the December 2017 Tübingen Cause-Effect Pairs benchmark version — using the eligible pairwise tasks with expert-assigned directions — Causal Mosaic reported about <strong>83% unweighted accuracy</strong> (Wu &amp; Fukumizu 2020). But note the shape of the trick: you escape the ceiling only by <em>assuming</em> non-Gaussianity, additivity, independence, or related structure. Some implications of these assumptions can be checked against data, but they cannot be fully established from finite observational samples. <StatusChip status={"hint"} label={"PROMISING / works under assumptions."} printLabel={"promising"} /></p>
<p>Which is why randomized assignment remains the benchmark design for internal validity when the intervention is assignable. A <em class="term">randomized controlled trial</em><TipNote text={String.raw`A randomized controlled trial assigns participants to conditions by chance so background causes are balanced on average.`} /> makes assignment independent of pre-assignment causes in the study population, up to chance imbalance. In an ideal trial with adherence, no interference, a well-defined treatment, complete follow-up, and appropriate analysis, assignment and treatment coincide closely enough to estimate intervention effects directly. With noncompliance, the cleanest identified quantity may instead be the effect of assignment; effects of treatment received require additional assumptions. When you can't randomize, economics' <em class="term">credibility revolution</em><TipNote text={String.raw`The credibility revolution in economics moved emphasis from clever regressions to research designs that approximate randomized experiments: instruments, discontinuities, and policy changes that assign exposure as-if at random.`} /> hunts for designs that approximate random assignment — instruments, discontinuities, policy changes. That program earned the <strong>2021 Nobel</strong> in economics for David Card, Joshua Angrist, and Guido Imbens (we'll spend real time here on <strong>Day 152</strong>). <StatusChip status={"ok"} label={"ESTABLISHED."} printLabel={"established"} /></p>
</div>

<div class="claim">
<div class="ctop">
<span class="cnum">Edge 03</span>
<StatusChip status={"hint"} label={"causal representation learning · promising program"} printLabel={"promising"} />
<StatusChip status={"bad"} label={"\"LLMs reason causally\" · task-dependent"} printLabel={"task-dependent"} />
</div>
<h3>Can the machines do it? Causal ML and the "causal parrot"</h3>
<p>The hottest and haziest edge. <strong><em class="term">Causal representation learning</em></strong><TipNote text={String.raw`Causal representation learning tries to learn useful causal variables from raw data such as pixels, text, or sensor streams.`} /> (Schölkopf et al., <em>Proceedings of the IEEE</em>, May 2021) asks the deep question: classical causal discovery assumes the variables are handed to you, but the real world arrives as pixels and words. Can a network <em>learn</em> the high-level causal variables — and would that buy robustness to <em class="term">distribution shift</em><TipNote text={String.raw`Distribution shift means the data a model sees after deployment differ from the data it learned from.`} />, which today's models often lack? It's a serious, active program whose biggest promises remain <StatusChip status={"hint"} label={"PROMISING HINT"} printLabel={"promising"} />, not yet delivered at scale.</p>
<p>Then the lightning rod: <strong>can large language models reason about cause and effect?</strong> Kıcıman, Ness, Sharma &amp; Tan (TMLR 2024; initial arXiv preprint 2023) reported GPT-4 hitting <strong>97% on a natural-language Tübingen pairwise causal-direction task</strong> — using textual variable descriptions rather than the numerical observations — plus strong counterfactual scores. A contemporaneous critique argued that LLMs <em>talk</em> causality without <em>being</em> causal — they recite causal facts marinating in their training text rather than performing Pearl-style inference. Results are strongly task-dependent. Models can retrieve familiar causal facts and achieve high scores on semantically rich benchmarks, yet perform poorly when linguistic and world-knowledge shortcuts are removed or when causal mechanisms must be composed and transferred to novel settings. Recall the <a href="/days/001-what-is-knowledge/"><strong>Day 1 Gettier trap</strong></a> in a new guise: an LLM that outputs a true causal claim for reasons that have nothing to do with the causal structure is right, but does it <em>know</em>? Verdict on "LLMs reason causally in Pearl's sense": <StatusChip status={"bad"} label={"Task-dependent; not established as general causal reasoning."} printLabel={"task-dependent"} /> Useful for <em>setting up</em> a causal analysis; not established as a general causal reasoner. DoWhy and EconML are genuine tools in the modern ecosystem: DoWhy emphasizes graph-based identification, assumptions, and refutation; EconML emphasizes treatment-effect estimation with orthogonal and machine-learning methods. The sales pitch that causal AI will soon replace ordinary correlational ML is still ahead of the evidence.</p>
</div>

<SectionEyebrow>Open questions</SectionEyebrow>

## What's genuinely unsettled

<ul class="clean">
<li><strong>Is there a "right" theory of causation at all,</strong> or are counterfactual, probabilistic, INUS, mechanistic, and interventionist accounts each capturing a different facet — with none reducible to the others? No single account is generally accepted as resolving every major class of counterexample, including early and late preemption, overdetermination, omissions, and prevention.</li>
<li><strong>How far can we trust faithfulness?</strong> The assumption that real systems do not hide causal paths through exact cancellations is convenient. Some implications can be checked against data, but faithfulness cannot be fully established from finite observational samples — and feedback, regulation, and homeostasis can make it questionable in some biological systems.</li>
<li><strong>Can causal variables be learned from raw data</strong> (pixels, language) rather than handed to the algorithm — and is that even well-posed, since the "right" carve-up of the world into variables may itself be perspective-dependent?</li>
<li><strong>Do large models build internal causal world-models,</strong> or only ever statistics of causal talk? The answer reaches straight into <strong>Days 138–145</strong> and the question of whether prediction can ever amount to understanding.</li>
<li><strong>Where do the arrows come from?</strong> Every method here needs <em>some</em> causal input — a graph, an assumption, an experiment. Hume's ghost still asks whether that input is ever read off the world, or always brought to it.</li>
</ul>
