## Classifying routes to a tipping point

Start with the deepest problem in the classic warning signals: they are *generic to a fault*. Rising variance and autocorrelation appear before almost any kind of tipping, which is their strength — they work everywhere — but also their weakness: they cannot tell you which *kind* of tipping is coming, and so they cannot tell you what the new world will be like. Will the system jump abruptly to a far-off state? Start oscillating wildly? Slide gently into a new regime? The generic signal shrugs at all three.

In 2021, a team led by Thomas Bury, with Chris Bauch, Madhur Anand, Marten Scheffer, and Tim Lenton, published a result in *PNAS*. Their method uses a mathematical property from Day 9: near a tipping point, many systems can be approximated by one of a small number of templates called <Term as="em">normal forms</Term><TipNote text={"Normal forms are simplified mathematical templates that capture a system's local behavior near a bifurcation."} /> — including the fold, Hopf, and transcritical bifurcations. Bury's team trained a <Term as="em">convolutional-recurrent neural network</Term><TipNote text={"A convolutional-recurrent neural network combines local pattern detection with memory for sequences."} /> on hundreds of thousands of simulated time series drawn from these templates, teaching it to distinguish several routes to transition.

Their network detected tipping points in systems it was *never trained on* — including real climate and ecological data and a thermoacoustic experiment — more sensitively and with fewer false alarms than the classic indicators. It also classified the *type* of bifurcation, distinguishing a sudden jump, an oscillation, or a gentler shift. A 2025 follow-up (Zhuge, Li &amp; Chen, *Royal Society Open Science*) extended the approach to *irregularly sampled* time series, a common feature of real-world monitoring and a limitation of the original method.

## The digital twin that visits the far side

Detecting that a transition is near is one task. A 2020–2021 program asks whether a machine trained only on a system's normal regime can predict its critical transition and post-tipping dynamics. The tool is <Term as="em">reservoir computing</Term><TipNote text={"Reservoir computing uses a fixed recurrent dynamical system to transform input streams, then trains a simpler readout to predict the target."} />, a recurrent-network method that can learn chaotic dynamics from raw data without an explicit model of the underlying physics.

In a 2021 *Physical Review Research* paper, Ling-Wei Kong, Ying-Cheng Lai, and colleagues supplied a reservoir computer with both the system's behavior and the slowly drifting *parameter* pushing it toward the transition — a "parameter input channel." Trained only on data from the safe regime, the machine then **extrapolated** to unseen parameter values, predicting where the critical transition lay and statistical properties of the transient state before final collapse. Follow-up work (Dhruvit Patel and Edward Ott, *Chaos*, 2023) used machine learning to "anticipate tipping points and extrapolate to post-tipping dynamics" of changing systems. Kong's group describes such trained reservoirs as <Term as="em">digital twins</Term><TipNote text={"Digital twins are data-linked virtual models used to represent and test a physical or dynamical counterpart."} /> of nonlinear systems: data-driven stand-ins that can be tested beyond the observed regime in software.

<BlockQuote>
The aim is a learned surrogate that can be tested beyond the observed regime without perturbing the real system.
</BlockQuote>

The method works on benchmark chaotic systems, but those systems are relatively low-dimensional, well sampled, and cleanly measured. Climate data are high-dimensional, sparse, and <Term as="em">non-stationary</Term><TipNote text={"Non-stationary data have statistical properties that change over time rather than remaining stable."} /> in ways that can defeat the assumption that future dynamics resemble the training regime. The same community has published "Catch-22" analyses of reservoir computing's limits. Results on toy systems are promising; a trustworthy digital twin of the AMOC does not yet exist, and real-Earth applications remain unproven.
</ContentSection>

## How a pattern lets a system dodge the edge

Another result questions whether a spatially extended ecosystem must collapse all at once after crossing a threshold. In 2021, Max Rietkerk and colleagues argued in *Science* that spatial structure can change this outcome.

Instead of collapsing all at once, a spatially extended system under stress can **reorganize into a pattern** — the spots, stripes, and labyrinths visible in drylands around the world. These are <Term as="em">Turing patterns</Term><TipNote text={"Turing patterns are self-organized spatial patterns produced when interacting processes amplify some wavelengths and suppress others."} />, the self-organized structures Alan Turing predicted in 1952. Ecologists have often read such patterns as warning signs of imminent collapse; Rietkerk's analysis shows that they can instead indicate **resilience**. By breaking into patches, a system can persist under more stress than a uniform version. It can replace abrupt collapse with an often reversible retreat into pattern: a ramp rather than a cliff.

If the framework generalizes, some ecosystems and Earth-system components classified as tipping-prone may be more resilient than models without spatial dynamics imply. The argument combines mathematics with field observations, but it is a review and theoretical framework, not a universal law. Its applicability to ice sheets, the Amazon, and the whole Earth system remains open. It also complicates warning signals, because a pattern can indicate either approaching collapse or persistence. The idea is promising; its general scope is unknown.
</ContentSection>

## Reading the Earth's resilience from orbit

Classic warning signals were developed on comparatively small datasets, including whole-lake experiments and ice cores. Since 2020, researchers have applied them across decades of satellite imagery, estimating resilience over the land surface pixel by pixel. The logic is unchanged from Day 9: a system losing resilience recovers more slowly, so its autocorrelation rises.

In 2021, Niklas Boers found statistically significant early-warning signals across *eight independent* observational fingerprints of the AMOC, concluding the Atlantic circulation may have drifted, over the last century, "from relatively stable conditions to a point close to a critical transition" (*Nature Climate Change*). The same year, Boers and Rypdal reported critical slowing down suggesting the western Greenland Ice Sheet sits near a tipping point (*PNAS*). In 2022, two papers — Smith, Traxl &amp; Boers in *Nature Climate Change*, and Forzieri and colleagues in *Nature* — used satellite vegetation indices to report that forests across much of the planet have been **losing resilience since the early 2000s**, especially in the tropics, even as some northern forests gained it. Tim Lenton's group proposed a permanent "*resilience sensing system for the biosphere*" (2022).

<Aside>
<BlockTitle>The reliability problem — read this before you panic or relax</BlockTitle>

These results are easy to over-read. A 2024 study in *Nature Ecology &amp; Evolution* showed that satellite resilience estimates become *unreliable in high-biomass regions* such as dense tropical forest, partly because thick canopies saturate the optical signal. Other 2023–2025 work found that data gaps, outliers, and instrument choice can distort the indicators, and a 2025 *Nature Climate Change* analysis argued that early-warning signals for climate tipping points can be fundamentally **ambiguous**. The global pattern warrants attention, but rising autocorrelation in one noisy pixel is a hypothesis, not a verdict.
</Aside>

The AMOC evidence includes a substantial counterpoint. A 2025 *Nature* study argued that wind-driven upwelling in the Southern Ocean may stabilize the Atlantic circulation enough to make an outright collapse *this century* unlikely. Both findings are peer reviewed, and their disagreement reflects the current state of the science. The 2026 summary is that observation-based estimates of resilience loss now operate at planetary scale, while converting those signals into confident claims about *which* system tips *when* remains contested.
</ContentSection>

## Setting off the cascades we want

The final program asks whether reinforcing loops can be deliberately *seeded* to accelerate beneficial change. This is the science of <Term as="em">positive tipping points</Term><TipNote text={"Positive tipping points are thresholds after which self-reinforcing processes accelerate a beneficial change."} />. It has become an active part of systems research since 2020 because holding warming near 1.5°C requires emissions to fall faster than gradual policy has so far delivered; the premise is that self-accelerating adoption can help close that gap.

Ilona Otto, Jonathan Donges, and colleagues convened experts in a 2020 *PNAS* paper to identify <Term as="em">social tipping elements</Term><TipNote text={"Social tipping elements are subsystems where a modest push can trigger rapid self-reinforcing change."} /> — leverage-rich subsystems where a modest push could trigger rapid change toward carbon neutrality. Their candidates included energy markets, financial divestment, carbon-neutral cities, norms and values, education, and *information disclosure*, linking back to Appendix I's leverage ladder. Tim Lenton's group followed with a framework for "*operationalising*" these points (2022) and a method to identify them while "avoiding wishful thinking about their existence." Falling solar and battery costs provide a concrete example: in some sectors, clean energy has crossed cost thresholds that produce self-reinforcing adoption along an <Term as="em">S-curve</Term><TipNote text={"An S-curve describes slow initial adoption, rapid growth, and eventual saturation."} />.

The hype filter here is unusual, because the object is partly *normative* — a goal as much as a fact. Some positive tipping points are demonstrably real and already past (the UK's exit from coal; solar's cost crossover). Others are hopeful conjectures about social systems whose contagion dynamics we understand far less well than a melting ice sheet — and critics (in replies to Otto et al.) warned that treating society like a physical system risks ignoring conflict, politics, and "how change actually happens." Some cases are established; the general program remains promising but partly aspirational.
</ContentSection>

## What remains unresolved

- **Can any method give a trustworthy lead time?** Every program here improves *detection*; none has convincingly cracked *timing* for a real, half-observed Earth-system component. This is the field's central, stubborn gap.
- **Will AI predictors survive contact with the real Earth?** Normal-form networks and reservoir twins shine on clean systems. The open test is whether they hold up on sparse, noisy, non-stationary planetary data — or quietly overfit the past.
- **How far does the spatial escape hatch reach?** Rietkerk's evasion is demonstrated for drylands. Does it extend to forests, ice, ocean circulation — or are those the systems where the cliff is real after all?
- **Can a falling pattern be told from a rising one?** If spatial patterns can mean either imminent collapse *or* resilient persistence, every spatial early-warning signal needs a way to distinguish them. Several 2024–25 groups are studying that problem.
- **Do social systems really tip like physical ones?** The positive-tipping program borrows mathematics used for physical systems. Where that analogy holds — and where politics and conflict break it — remains under study.
</ContentSection>

<Recap>
<PanelTitle>The frontier in three sentences</PanelTitle>

**New capability:** since 2020, machine learning (normal-form-trained deep nets; parameter-aware reservoir "digital twins") and planetary-scale satellite analysis have begun classifying the *type* of tipping, modeling post-transition dynamics, and mapping resilience loss across the Earth.

**Counterpoint:** Rietkerk's work shows that spatial self-organization can let some systems *evade* catastrophic tipping, while positive-tipping research asks whether reinforcing loops can accelerate decarbonization.

**Calibration:** detection methods are improving, but forecasting a specific collapse *date* for a real system remains unsolved, and the newer tools still rely heavily on clean simulations rather than planetary data — promise, not yet proof.
</Recap>
