## Odd matter

Start with a rule so basic it feels like cheating to question it. Newton's third law: every action has an equal and opposite reaction. If I push you, you push me back, exactly as hard. In ordinary matter this is ironclad, and it's the hidden reason equilibrium physics works the way it does. But step into the world of *living and driven* systems and the law quietly fails. A predator chases prey; the prey flees the predator — but the prey is not "pushing back" on the predator with an equal, opposite chase. A conformist in a crowd moves toward you; a contrarian moves away. These interactions are *non-reciprocal*: A responds to B in a way B does *not* mirror. And it turns out that this single, generic feature — almost unavoidable whenever energy is flowing through a system — unlocks entire phases of matter that are flatly forbidden at equilibrium.

This is the thesis of one of the most influential complexity-physics papers of the decade: Michel Fruchart, Ryo Hanai, Peter Littlewood, and Vincenzo Vitelli's **"Non-reciprocal phase transitions"** (*Nature*, 2021). Their key discovery is a new *kind* of transition. In familiar physics, when a system "orders," it picks a direction and freezes there (a magnet points north). Fruchart and colleagues showed that non-reciprocal systems can instead enter a phase where the broken symmetry is **dynamically restored** — the system never settles, but chases its own tail forever, producing spontaneous *travelling* and *oscillating* states. The mathematics that governs the onset of this behavior is borrowed from a surprising place: the "exceptional points" of non-Hermitian quantum mechanics, where eigenvalues collide and the usual rules buckle.

Non-reciprocity links flocking, active matter, synchronization, neuroscience, and social dynamics.

Why is this potentially landscape-changing? Because equilibrium statistical mechanics was built for systems that obey detailed balance and the third law. Living systems don't. Non-reciprocal physics begins a theory of phase transitions for driven biological matter.

### Starfish embryos, frozen mid-dance

The theory landed in the real world in spectacular fashion. In **"Odd dynamics of living chiral crystals"** (*Nature*, 2022), Tzer Han Tan, Jörn Dunkel, Nikta Fakhri and colleagues took thousands of *starfish embryos* — each a tiny ball spinning gently in one direction — and let them pack together on a surface. They spontaneously crystallized into a vast ordered lattice spanning thousands of organisms and lasting for hours. But this was no ordinary crystal. Because every embryo spins the same way (it is *chiral*), the lattice exhibited **odd elasticity**: when you deform it, it pushes back in a *sideways* direction, and — astonishingly — it can do net *work* over a deformation cycle, extracting energy from the embryos' rotation. A passive, equilibrium solid (a normal crystal, a steel beam) can never do that; the energy bookkeeping forbids it. The starfish crystal sustained slow, self-powered chiral waves rippling across it, a whole "breathing" with no conductor — emergence again, but of a kind that was mathematically impossible until you let go of the third law.

## Topological defects as the architects of a body

Here is a question that sounds like it should belong to genetics but is turning out to belong, at least partly, to *physics*: when a featureless ball of cells decides *where* to grow a head, what makes that spot special? A startling line of work says the answer can be written in the language of liquid crystals — the same physics in your laptop screen.

Inside many tissues, elongated cells and their internal protein fibres line up like the molecules in a liquid crystal, forming an orientation field. And orientation fields, mathematically, are forced to contain *topological defects* — special points where the alignment becomes undefined and swirls around itself (think of the crown of hairs on the back of your head, where the hairs spiral out from a single point that can't "choose" a direction). These defects are not bugs; they are unavoidable features of the geometry. The frontier claim is that **biology has learned to read them as addresses.**

In **"Topological defects in the nematic order of actin fibres as organization centres of *Hydra* morphogenesis"** (*Nature Physics*, 2021), Yael Maroudas-Sacks, Kinneret Keren and colleagues watched a *Hydra* — a small freshwater animal that can regenerate its entire body from a fragment — rebuild itself. They found that the head, tentacles, and foot reliably emerge at the locations of specific defects in the actin orientation field. The defect comes *first*; the body part grows where the physics put the singularity. A companion theory paper (*Science Advances*, 2022) supplied the mechanism: a "+1" defect focuses mechanical stress and acts like a mechanical morphogen, buckling the sheet outward into a protrusion.

## Machines that learn with no processor

Appendix I ended with the slime mold and the ant colony computing without brains. This is that idea's most radical 2024 descendant — and it might rewrite how we think about both learning and hardware. We assume that to "learn" you need a computer: a central processor running an algorithm, holding a model, adjusting weights. The physical-learning program asks a heretical question: **what if learning is just something certain physical systems *do*, the way a river finds the sea — no processor, no algorithm, no model of itself anywhere?**

In **"Machine learning without a processor: Emergent learning in a nonlinear analog network"** (*PNAS*, 2024), Sam Dillavou, Andrea Liu, Douglas Durian and colleagues built exactly this: a physical network of nonlinear electronic components — just resistors, diodes, and wires, no chip — that *learns* to perform nonlinear classification tasks. The trick is a scheme called *contrastive local learning*: the network is shown an input and nudged toward the right output, and each component adjusts itself using *only* information physically available at its own two ends. No component knows the task. No component knows the network. There is no global controller. And yet the system as a whole descends an error landscape and learns — the learning is a genuinely **emergent collective property of the physics**, distributed across thousands of dumb parts.

Even the texture of how it learns echoes "real" neural networks: the authors report it corrects errors in a natural order — first the average, then the slope, then the finer curvature of the target function — a physical cousin of the "spectral bias" seen in artificial networks. ## Anthrobots: cells that build a self you've never had

Now one of the strangest and most genuinely unsettling results of the decade. We think of the cells in your body as having fixed jobs — a tracheal cell makes mucus and cilia, forever, because that's what tracheal cells do. The frontier finding is that this is wildly underselling them. Freed from the body and its instructions, your own cells will spontaneously build *entirely new organisms with anatomies and behaviors that have never existed*.

In **"Motile Living Biobots Self-Construct from Adult Human Somatic Progenitor Seed Cells"** (*Advanced Science*, 2023), Gizem Gumuskaya and Michael Levin took adult human bronchial cells — ordinary airway cells, no genetic engineering whatsoever — and simply changed their environment. The cells self-assembled into tiny spherical multicellular organisms the team calls *Anthrobots*: between a third of a millimeter and half a millimeter across, covered in cilia they used as oars to swim, surviving for weeks. Different Anthrobots spontaneously developed different shapes and different movement styles. And in a startling demonstration, clusters of them ("superbots") laid across a scratch in a sheet of cultured human neurons triggered the neurons to **regrow across the wound** — a repair the neurons would not otherwise perform.

This builds directly on the earlier *Xenobots* (made from frog embryo cells, which famously discovered a form of self-replication by pushing loose cells into piles). But Anthrobots raise the stakes: they are made from *adult, wild-type, human* cells, the same cells in your own airway right now.

Why it matters: it suggests a latent "software of life", a capacity for cells to build novel functional anatomies. Status: established. Where to keep your skepticism: medical use is early, and cellular "agency" or "intelligence" remains philosophically loaded. Status: contested.
</ContentSection>

## Emergence inside the machine: grokking & the physics of learning

The main lesson hinted that the "sudden new abilities" of large AI models might be a measurement artifact (the Day 139 "mirage" debate). That's one important story. But there's a second, deeper, and largely *separate* body of recent work that treats the inside of a training neural network as a genuine physical system — one that undergoes real phase transitions, with order parameters and critical points, as it learns. Here the emergence looks much less like a mirage.

The cleanest example is a phenomenon with the wonderful name *grokking* (Power et al., 2022, and a wave of mechanistic follow-ups). Train a small network on a crisp mathematical task — say, modular arithmetic. At first it simply *memorizes* the training examples: perfect on what it's seen, useless on anything new. And then you keep training, long past the point where it seems anything could change... and **suddenly, often thousands of steps later, it "groks"** — it abruptly snaps into true understanding, generalizing perfectly to problems it never saw. Researchers cracked it open and found a real, mechanistic story: the network is quietly building an *efficient, structured* internal algorithm (it literally discovers the trigonometry of modular arithmetic) underneath the memorized lookup table, and the moment that efficient circuit outcompetes the clumsy memorization, generalization snaps on like a switch. The transition is sharp, like water freezing. Watch the characteristic signature:

The Quantization Model of Neural Scaling proposes that network knowledge comes in discrete chunks, while singular learning theory reframes training as Bayesian phase transitions. Both make learning look less like smooth accumulation and more like structure crystallizing. Status: promising.

The thread we hand forward: where the Day 139 "mirage" paper urges caution about *emergence* as an illusion of metrics, this body of work suggests that *some* emergence inside learning systems is mechanistically real — sharp, structured, and explicable. Both can be true, and untangling them is one of the live frontiers of AI science. (We'll meet it again in full on **Days 138–145**.)

## Disordered hyperuniformity: the order hiding inside disorder

Appendix I's complexity hump told us the interesting stuff lives *between* perfect order and perfect randomness. Hyperuniformity is the discovery of a genuinely new category of matter living in exactly that gap — and it's one of the most quietly important ideas in recent materials physics. Here's the puzzle it solves. We have two clean categories of arrangement: **crystals** (perfectly ordered, every atom on a grid) and **disordered** stuff like liquids and glasses (no long-range pattern). Hyperuniformity is a third thing that shouldn't be allowed to exist: arrangements that look locally *random and disordered* — no grid, no obvious pattern — yet are *globally* as smooth and uniform as a crystal, with the long-wavelength density fluctuations of a perfect lattice completely suppressed.

Salvatore Torquato, who has driven this field, defines it crisply: in a *disordered hyperuniform* material, the big, long-range density variations that any normal liquid has are *anomalously quenched* — squashed to zero — while the local arrangement stays as messy and isotropic as a fluid. It's "hidden order": disorder up close, crystalline discipline at scale. Nature uses it: the light-sensing cones in a chicken's eye are packed this way, and the trick produces remarkable *stealthy* photonic materials that block specific wavelengths of light from any direction — something ordinary crystals (which have preferred directions) cannot do. ## When connections themselves can be switched

For decades, network science (which gets its own day — **Day 12**) drew the world as dots joined by lines: people and friendships, neurons and synapses, always *pairwise*. The frontier insight of the 2020s is that this misses something essential. Real interactions are often *many-bodied*: three proteins that only function as a trio, a group conversation that is more than the sum of its one-on-ones. Capturing this requires *higher-order networks* — built not just from edges (pairs) but from triangles, tetrahedra, and higher shapes (the objects mathematicians call *simplicial complexes* and *hypergraphs*). And once you allow them, genuinely new emergent dynamics appear.

The most striking recent example is **triadic percolation** (Sun, Radicchi, Kurths & Bianconi, *Nature Communications*, 2023). Recall from Appendix I that ordinary percolation has a single, static tipping point: crank up connectivity and a giant cluster snaps into being, once, and stays. Triadic percolation breaks that placidity by adding a twist drawn from real systems (like the brain, where one neuron can *regulate* the connection between two others): here a node can **switch a link on or off** between two other nodes. Now the connectivity is no longer fixed — it's *dynamic*, links blinking in and out as the network reconfigures itself. The result is remarkable: the giant cluster no longer settles. It can **oscillate** in size, period-double, and even tumble into full-blown *chaos* — the size of the connected world flickering unpredictably from one moment to the next.

## Contested frontier claims

The brief for this appendix explicitly asked for the provocative and the unverified — so we close with four ideas that are scientifically *active* but very much not settled. These are included precisely so you can practice the hype filter on live ammunition. Each comes paired with its strongest published counter-argument.

Frontier 01

contested — on timing

### Is the Atlantic's great current about to collapse?

The AMOC — the ocean circulation that keeps Europe mild — is a textbook tipping system, and complexity science's *early-warning signal* toolkit (watching for "critical slowing down": a system getting sluggish and wobbly as it nears a cliff) has been turned on it. A widely-covered 2023 statistical study (Ditlevsen & Ditlevsen, *Nature Communications*) put a central estimate for collapse at around **2057** (with a wide 2025–2095 window), and a 2024 physics-based study (van Westen et al., *Science Advances*) found a concrete warning indicator pointing the same way. **The counter-punch:** a 2025 *Nature* study (Baker et al.) tested 34 climate models and found the AMOC *resilient* — weakening but not collapsing this century, kept alive by winds in the Southern Ocean — concluding a collapse is "unlikely." The early-warning *method* is a real complexity-science contribution; the specific collapse date is genuinely disputed and the models disagree.

Ditlevsen & Ditlevsen, *Nat. Commun.* 14:4254 (2023) · van Westen et al., *Sci. Adv.* 10:eadk1189 (2024) · Baker et al., *Nature* 638:987 (2025).

Frontier 02

contested — universality, not existence

### Do living things obey a universal "3/4 power" law?

One of complexity's most celebrated claims is that an animal's metabolic rate scales as its mass to the 3/4 power — supposedly a universal law flowing from the fractal geometry of blood vessels (West, Brown & Enquist). It's beautiful, and it's increasingly under fire. A 2022 review (Glazier, *Proc. R. Soc. B*) marshals *hundreds* of studies documenting that the exponent is **not** a fixed 3/4 but varies systematically — across species, activity levels, and conditions, ranging anywhere from roughly 0.1 to 1.6. The pattern of metabolic scaling is real; the claim that it's a single *universal* constant set by vascular geometry does not survive the full breadth of biological data. A clean cautionary tale about mistaking an *average* for a *law*.

Glazier, "Variable metabolic scaling breaks the law," *Proc. R. Soc. B* 289:20221605 (2022).

Frontier 03

contested / hype

### A "second law" for information — or physics fan-fiction?

Melvin Vopson has proposed a *second law of infodynamics* (*AIP Advances*, 2022–2023): the claim that the "information entropy" of information-bearing systems stays constant or *decreases* over time — the mirror image of ordinary thermodynamics — with downstream speculations that information might be a form of dark matter and that this is "evidence" we live in a simulation. It's published in a legitimate journal, which is why it's here. But it sits far outside mainstream physics, has no independent confirmation, and the simulation-hypothesis leap is not testable physics. We met its cousin — Vopson's "mass–energy–information equivalence" — being flagged as fringe back on **Day 7**. Treat as a vivid specimen of how a provocative "new law" propagates faster than its evidence. **Skepticism: maximal.**

Vopson & Lepadatu, *AIP Advances* 12:075310 (2022); Vopson, *AIP Advances* 13:105308 (2023). See also Hossenfelder's public rebuttals.

Frontier 04

contested — on scope & framing

### Are your cells, in some real sense, thinking?

Michael Levin (the Anthrobots architect from §4) argues for a sweeping reframing: that *bioelectric* signaling among cells — the voltages and ion flows that pattern tissues — implements a form of goal-directed *"basal cognition,"* a rewritable memory and problem-solving capacity running far below the brain, with "collective intelligence" as a unifying principle across all of biology (*Animal Cognition*, 2023; *Communications Biology*, 2024). The underlying *bioelectric mechanisms* are real, increasingly well-documented, and genuinely exciting — planarian flatworms really do store head-shape "memories" in their bioelectric state. **What's contested** is the strong cognitive language: whether calling a cell collective "intelligent" or "cognitive" is a deep insight or a stretched metaphor is a live and heated debate. A perfect note to end on, because it drags us right back to the question that opened the whole day — *when is a new high-level description (here, "cognition") carving nature at a joint, and when is it a re-description of the parts?* Sixty years after Anderson, that question is still the one that matters most.

Levin, *Animal Cognition* 26:1865 (2023); McMillen & Levin, *Commun. Biol.* 7:378 (2024).

## The frontier scorecard

Eight dispatches, eight honest verdicts. This is the whole appendix compressed into the hype filter — a snapshot of one corner of science in mid-2026, with the explicit understanding that several of these labels will move over the next decade.

- Non-reciprocal phase transitions & odd matter — new phases of matter once Newton's third law breaks (Nature 2021/2022). Established · expanding
- Topological defects as morphogenetic organizers — geometry marking where body parts grow (Nat. Phys. 2021). Established correlation
- Physical learning machines — networks that learn with no processor (PNAS 2024). Promising hint
- Anthrobots — adult human cells self-build novel motile organisms (Adv. Sci. 2023). Phenomenon established
- Grokking & the physics of learning — real phase transitions inside training nets (2022–2025). Promising hint
- Disordered hyperuniformity — a third category of order, now in quantum matter (PNAS 2025). Established · spreading
- Triadic / higher-order network dynamics — chaos in percolation; topology shapes dynamics (2023–2025). Math established
- AMOC collapse timing · metabolic 3/4 law · infodynamics · cell "cognition" — the contested four. Contested / hype
</ContentSection>
