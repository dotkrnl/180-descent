## Odd matter

Start with a rule so basic it feels like cheating to question it. Newton's third law: every action has an equal and opposite reaction. In fundamental Newtonian mechanics, momentum conservation is not being repealed. But in many *living and driven* systems, the effective interactions inside the observed subsystem can become <Term as="em">nonreciprocal</Term><TipNote text={"Nonreciprocal means A's effective influence on B is not simply mirrored by B's influence on A."} /> because momentum or energy is exchanged with the environment. A predator-prey chase is a useful analogy, not a literal force-law violation. A conformist in a crowd moves toward you; a contrarian moves away. These interactions are nonreciprocal: A responds to B in a way B does *not* mirror. And in many open, driven systems with asymmetric effective interactions, that feature can unlock phases of matter that are forbidden at equilibrium.

This is the thesis of an influential complexity-physics paper: Michel Fruchart, Ryo Hanai, Peter Littlewood, and Vincenzo Vitelli's **"Non-reciprocal phase transitions"** (*Nature*, 2021). Their key discovery is a new *kind* of transition. In familiar physics, when a system "orders," it picks a direction and freezes there (a magnet points north). Fruchart and colleagues showed that nonreciprocal systems can instead enter a phase where the broken symmetry is **dynamically restored** — the system never settles, but chases its own tail forever, producing spontaneous *travelling* and *oscillating* states. The mathematics that governs the onset of this behavior is borrowed from a surprising place: the <Term as="em">exceptional points</Term><TipNote text={"An exceptional point is a special point in a non-Hermitian system where eigenvalues and eigenvectors merge."} /> of <Term as="em">non-Hermitian</Term><TipNote text={"Non-Hermitian here means the mathematical operator does not have the symmetry that guarantees real energies and ordinary mode behavior in closed quantum systems."} /> quantum mechanics, where eigenvalues collide and the usual rules buckle.

Nonreciprocity links flocking, active matter, synchronization, neuroscience, and social dynamics.

Why is this potentially landscape-changing? Because equilibrium statistical mechanics was built for systems that obey detailed balance and reciprocal effective interactions. Living systems often don't. Nonreciprocal physics begins a theory of phase transitions for driven biological matter.

### Starfish embryos, frozen mid-dance

The theory landed in the real world in spectacular fashion. In **"Odd dynamics of living chiral crystals"** (*Nature*, 2022), Tzer Han Tan, Jörn Dunkel, Nikta Fakhri and colleagues took thousands of *starfish embryos* — each a tiny ball spinning gently in one direction — and let them pack together on a surface. They spontaneously crystallized into a vast ordered lattice spanning thousands of organisms and lasting for hours. But this was no ordinary crystal. Because every embryo spins the same way (it is <Term as="em">chiral</Term><TipNote text={"Chiral means an object or motion has a handedness: it is not equivalent to its mirror image."} />), the lattice exhibited <Term as="em">odd elasticity</Term><TipNote text={"Odd elasticity is an active-material response where deformation can produce sideways forces and net work over a cycle."} />: when you deform it, it pushes back in a *sideways* direction, and — astonishingly — it can do net *work* over a deformation cycle, extracting energy from the embryos' rotation. A passive, equilibrium solid (a normal crystal, a steel beam) can never do that; the energy bookkeeping forbids it. The starfish crystal sustained slow, self-powered chiral waves rippling across it, a whole "breathing" with no conductor — emergence again, but of a kind that was unavailable until the system was treated as open, active, and nonreciprocal.

## Topological defects as the architects of a body

Here is a question that sounds like it should belong to genetics but is turning out to belong, at least partly, to *physics*: when a featureless ball of cells decides *where* to grow a head, what makes that spot special? A startling line of work says the answer can be written in the language of liquid crystals — the same physics in your laptop screen.

Inside many tissues, elongated cells and their internal protein fibres line up like the molecules in a liquid crystal, forming an <Term as="em">orientation field</Term><TipNote text={"An orientation field assigns a local direction or alignment to each point in a tissue or material."} />. And orientation fields, mathematically, are forced to contain <Term as="em">topological defects</Term><TipNote text={"A topological defect is a point or line where an otherwise smooth pattern of alignment becomes undefined or singular."} /> — special points where the alignment becomes undefined and swirls around itself (think of the crown of hairs on the back of your head, where the hairs spiral out from a single point that can't "choose" a direction). These defects are not bugs; they are unavoidable features of the geometry. The frontier claim is that **biology has learned to read them as addresses.**

In **"Topological defects in the nematic order of actin fibres as organization centres of *Hydra* morphogenesis"** (*Nature Physics*, 2021), Yael Maroudas-Sacks, Kinneret Keren and colleagues watched a *Hydra* — a small freshwater animal that can regenerate its entire body from a fragment — rebuild itself. They found that defects in the actin orientation field precede and predict organizer and body sites. Newer topology-manipulation experiments strengthen the case for a causal organizer role, although the molecular-mechanical chain is still being resolved. The body part grows where the physics put the singularity. A companion theory paper (*Science Advances*, 2022) supplied one proposed mechanism: a "+1" defect focuses mechanical stress and acts like a mechanical morphogen, buckling the sheet outward into a protrusion.

## Machines that learn without gradient computation

Appendix I ended with the slime mold and the ant colony computing without brains. This is that idea's most radical 2024 descendant — and it might rewrite how we think about both learning and hardware. We assume that to "learn" you need a processor calculating gradients and sending weight updates. The physical-learning program asks a heretical question: **what if some of that update rule can be embodied locally in the physics itself?**

In **"Machine learning without a processor: Emergent learning in a nonlinear analog network"** (*PNAS*, 2024), Sam Dillavou, Andrea Liu, Douglas Durian and colleagues built a physical network of **32 trainable twin edges** using nonlinear transistor circuits. It learned classification and regression tasks through a scheme called <Term as="em">contrastive local learning</Term><TipNote text={"Contrastive local learning compares local signals between a free state and a nudged or constrained state so each trainable element can update using local information."} />: the network is shown an input and nudged toward the right output, and each trainable element adjusts using information available at its own terminals. External circuitry supplied examples, labels, input control, analog-to-digital and digital-to-analog conversion, and a global freeze/unfreeze signal. The key result is narrower but still powerful: no central processor calculated the weight gradients. The system's learning rule was distributed through local analog updates.

Even the texture of how it learns echoes "real" neural networks: the authors report it corrects errors in a natural order — first the average, then the slope, then the finer curvature of the target function — a physical cousin of the <Term as="em">spectral bias</Term><TipNote text={"Spectral bias is the tendency of some neural networks to learn low-frequency or simpler components of a function before finer high-frequency detail."} /> seen in artificial networks. Here is a sketch of that descent: dozens of local elements, each obeying only its own neighborhood rule, collectively sliding an error downhill.

## Anthrobots: cells that self-assemble into motile biobots

Now one of the strangest results of the decade. Airway-derived progenitor cells normally participate in airway-specific tissue, yet under different culture conditions they can self-assemble into motile multicellular constructs. The frontier finding is that this is underselling them. Freed from the body and placed in a new environment, adult human airway cells can spontaneously organize into forms with shapes and behaviors not present in the original tissue.

In **"Motile Living Biobots Self-Construct from Adult Human Somatic Progenitor Seed Cells"** (*Advanced Science*, 2024; first published online in 2023), Gizem Gumuskaya and Michael Levin took adult human bronchial cells — ordinary airway-derived cells, no genetic engineering whatsoever — and changed their environment. The cells self-assembled into tiny motile multicellular biobots the team calls *Anthrobots*: approximately **30-500 micrometers** across, with cilia they used for movement, surviving for weeks. Different Anthrobots spontaneously developed different shapes and movement styles. In vitro, clusters of these constructs were associated with repair across a scratch in a cultured neural-cell monolayer.

This builds directly on the earlier *Xenobots* (made from frog embryo cells, which famously discovered a form of self-replication by pushing loose cells into piles). But Anthrobots raise the stakes: they are made from *adult, wild-type, human* cells, the same cells in your own airway right now.

Why it matters: it suggests a latent "software of life", a capacity for cells to build novel functional multicellular forms. <StatusChip status="ok" label={"established"} /> Where to keep your skepticism: medical use is early, and cellular "agency" or "intelligence" remains philosophically loaded. <StatusChip status="bad" label={"contested"} />
</ContentSection>

## Emergence inside the machine: grokking & the physics of learning

The main lesson hinted that the "sudden new abilities" of large AI models might be a measurement artifact (the Day 139 "mirage" debate). That's one important story. But there's a second, deeper, and largely *separate* body of recent work that treats the inside of a training neural network as a physical system — sometimes with phase-transition-style descriptions as it learns. Here the emergence looks less like a pure mirage, but the strongest thermodynamic claims are still model-specific and under active test.

The cleanest example is a phenomenon with the wonderful name <Term as="em">grokking</Term><TipNote text={"Grokking is delayed generalization: a model memorizes training examples first, then much later abruptly improves on unseen cases."} /> (Power et al., 2022, and a wave of mechanistic follow-ups). Train a small network on a crisp mathematical task — say, modular arithmetic. At first it simply *memorizes* the training examples: perfect on what it's seen, useless on anything new. And then you keep training, long past the point where it seems anything could change... and **suddenly, often thousands of steps later, it "groks"** — it abruptly shifts to systematic out-of-sample generalization. In particular modular-arithmetic analyses, researchers found the network building an efficient, structured internal algorithm related to trigonometric representations underneath the memorized lookup table, and the moment that efficient circuit outcompetes memorization, generalization snaps on like a switch. The curve is sharp, but whether grokking generally constitutes a universal physical phase transition remains open. Watch the characteristic signature:

Grokking is now a workhorse for a fast-growing "physics of learning." Two related frontier programs are worth knowing. The Quantization Model of Neural Scaling proposes that network knowledge comes in discrete chunks, while <Term as="em">singular learning theory</Term><TipNote text={"Singular learning theory studies learning systems whose parameter spaces have degeneracies or singularities, using tools from algebraic geometry and Bayesian statistics."} /> reframes training using Bayesian phase-transition language. The thread we hand forward: where the Day 139 "mirage" paper urges caution about *emergence* as an illusion of metrics, this body of work suggests that *some* emergence inside learning systems is mechanistically real — sharp, structured, and explicable in controlled settings. Both can be true, and untangling them is one of the live frontiers of AI science. (We'll meet it again in full on **Days 138–145**.)

## Disordered hyperuniformity: the order hiding inside disorder

Appendix I's complexity hump told us the interesting stuff lives *between* perfect order and perfect randomness. <Term as="em">Hyperuniformity</Term> is a structural property that lives in exactly that gap — and it's one of the most quietly important ideas in recent materials physics. All perfect crystals and perfect quasicrystals are hyperuniform, and so are certain disordered systems. The frontier case is **disordered hyperuniformity**: arrangements that look locally random — no grid, no obvious pattern — yet anomalously suppress density fluctuations at very large scales, with <Term as="em">S(k) → 0</Term><TipNote text={"S(k) is the structure factor, a Fourier-space measure of density variation; S(k) → 0 as k → 0 means very large-scale fluctuations are suppressed."} /> as k → 0. It is not a mutually exclusive third category beside crystals and disorder; it is a way disorder can carry hidden large-scale order.

Salvatore Torquato, who has driven this field, defines it crisply: in a *disordered hyperuniform* material, the big, long-range density variations that any normal liquid has are *anomalously quenched* in the long-wavelength limit, while the local arrangement stays messy and isotropic. It's "hidden order": disorder up close, crystalline-style discipline at scale. Nature uses it: the light-sensing cones in a chicken's eye are packed this way, and the trick produces remarkable *stealthy* photonic materials that block specific wavelengths of light from any direction — something ordinary crystals (which have preferred directions) cannot do. ## When connections themselves can be switched

For decades, much of network science (which gets its own day — **Day 12**) drew the world as dots joined by lines: people and friendships, neurons and synapses, usually *pairwise*. Higher-order interactions did not originate in the 2020s, but higher-order network science has expanded rapidly in this decade, producing new results on triadic interactions, percolation, synchronization, and contagion. Real interactions are often *many-bodied*: three proteins that only function as a trio, a group conversation that is more than the sum of its one-on-ones. Capturing this requires <Term as="em">higher-order networks</Term><TipNote text={"Higher-order networks represent interactions among three or more entities directly, rather than reducing everything to pairwise links."} /> — built not just from edges (pairs) but from triangles, tetrahedra, and higher shapes (the objects mathematicians call <Term as="em">simplicial complexes</Term><TipNote text={"A simplicial complex represents higher-order relations with points, edges, triangles, tetrahedra, and their higher-dimensional analogues."} /> and <Term as="em">hypergraphs</Term><TipNote text={"A hypergraph is a network where one edge can connect any number of nodes, not just two."} />).

The most striking recent example is **triadic percolation** (Sun, Radicchi, Kurths & Bianconi, *Nature Communications*, 2023). Recall from Appendix I that ordinary percolation has a single, static tipping point: crank up connectivity and a giant cluster snaps into being, once, and stays. Triadic percolation breaks that placidity by adding a twist drawn from real systems (like the brain, where one neuron can *regulate* the connection between two others): here a node can **switch a link on or off** between two other nodes. Now the connectivity is no longer fixed — it's *dynamic*, links blinking in and out as the network reconfigures itself. The result is remarkable: the giant cluster no longer settles. It can **oscillate** in size, period-double, and even tumble into full-blown *chaos* — the size of the connected world flickering unpredictably from one moment to the next.

### Is the Atlantic's great current about to collapse?

The AMOC transports heat northward and strongly influences North Atlantic and European climate alongside atmospheric circulation, geography, and ocean-atmosphere coupling. It is also a textbook tipping system, and complexity science's <Term as="em">early-warning signal</Term><TipNote text={"An early-warning signal is a statistical pattern, such as rising variance or slower recovery, that may indicate a system is nearing a tipping point."} /> toolkit (watching for <Term as="em">critical slowing down</Term><TipNote text={"Critical slowing down means a system recovers more slowly from disturbances as it approaches certain tipping points."} />: a system getting sluggish and wobbly as it nears a cliff) has been turned on it. A widely-covered 2023 statistical study (Ditlevsen & Ditlevsen, *Nature Communications*) now reports, in its corrected version, a bootstrap mean around **2065** with a 95% interval of **2037–2109**, and a 2024 physics-based study (van Westen et al., *Science Advances*) found a concrete warning indicator pointing the same way. **The counter-punch:** a 2025 *Nature* study (Baker et al.) tested 34 climate models and found weakened overturning continuing through this century under the modeled scenarios, sustained by Southern Ocean upwelling. The early-warning *method* is a real complexity-science contribution; the specific collapse date is genuinely disputed and the models disagree.

Ditlevsen & Ditlevsen, *Nat. Commun.* 14:4254 (2023) · van Westen et al., *Sci. Adv.* 10:eadk1189 (2024) · Baker et al., *Nature* 638:987 (2025).
</Claim>

### Do living things obey a universal "3/4 power" law?

One of complexity's most celebrated claims is that an animal's metabolic rate scales as its mass to the 3/4 power — supposedly a universal law flowing from the fractal geometry of blood vessels (West, Brown & Enquist). It's beautiful, and it's increasingly under fire. A 2022 review (Glazier, *Proc. R. Soc. B*) marshals *hundreds* of studies documenting that the exponent is **not** a fixed 3/4 but varies systematically — across species, activity levels, and conditions, ranging anywhere from roughly 0.1 to 1.6. The pattern of metabolic scaling is real; the claim that it's a single *universal* constant set by vascular geometry does not survive the full breadth of biological data. A clean cautionary tale about mistaking an *average* for a *law*.

Glazier, "Variable metabolic scaling breaks the law," *Proc. R. Soc. B* 289:20221605 (2022).
</Claim>

### A "second law" for information — or physics fan-fiction?

Melvin Vopson has proposed a *second law of infodynamics* (*AIP Advances*, 2022–2023): the claim that the "information entropy" of information-bearing systems stays constant or *decreases* over time — the mirror image of ordinary thermodynamics — with downstream speculations that information might be a form of dark matter and that this is "evidence" we live in a simulation. It's published in a legitimate journal, which is why it's here. But it sits far outside mainstream physics, has no independent confirmation, and the simulation-hypothesis leap is not testable physics. We met its cousin — Vopson's "mass–energy–information equivalence" — being flagged as fringe back on **Day 7**. Treat as a vivid specimen of how a provocative "new law" propagates faster than its evidence. **Skepticism: maximal.**

Vopson & Lepadatu, *AIP Advances* 12:075310 (2022); Vopson, *AIP Advances* 13:105308 (2023). See also Hossenfelder's public rebuttals.
</Claim>

### Are your cells, in some real sense, thinking?

Michael Levin (the Anthrobots architect from §4) argues for a sweeping reframing: that <Term as="em">bioelectric</Term><TipNote text={"Bioelectric signals are voltage and ion-flow patterns used by cells and tissues, beyond the familiar electrical signaling of neurons."} /> signaling among cells — the voltages and ion flows that pattern tissues — implements a form of goal-directed <Term as="em">basal cognition</Term><TipNote text={"Basal cognition is the proposed idea that non-neural cells or cell groups can show primitive goal-directed problem solving."} />, a rewritable memory and problem-solving capacity running far below the brain, with "collective intelligence" as a unifying principle across all of biology (*Animal Cognition*, 2023; *Communications Biology*, 2024). The underlying *bioelectric mechanisms* are real, increasingly well-documented, and genuinely exciting: transient bioelectric perturbations in planarians can produce persistent altered regenerative outcomes, interpreted by Levin and colleagues as a form of pattern memory. **What's contested** is the strong cognitive language: whether calling a cell collective "intelligent" or "cognitive" is a deep insight or a stretched metaphor is a live and heated debate. A perfect note to end on, because it drags us right back to the question that opened the whole day — *when is a new high-level description (here, "cognition") carving nature at a joint, and when is it a re-description of the parts?* Sixty years after Anderson, that question is still the one that matters most.

Levin, *Animal Cognition* 26:1865 (2023); McMillen & Levin, *Commun. Biol.* 7:378 (2024).
