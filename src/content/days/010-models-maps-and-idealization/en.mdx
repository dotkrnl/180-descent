# Models, Maps &amp; Idealization

<HeroSubhead>A perfect map is a useless map. So what, exactly, is a good one for?</HeroSubhead>

<Day10StaticFigure kind="hero-map" locale="en" />

<Lead drop="I">n a single paragraph published in 1946, Jorge Luis Borges describes an empire whose cartographers grew so skilled, and so obsessed with exactitude, that they drew a map the size of the empire itself. It coincided with the territory point for point, lay over the country like a second skin, and became the most accurate map ever made. It was also worthless. Carroll supplied the even neater joke, a map on the scale of **one mile to the mile**; Borges supplied the ruin.</Lead>

The joke has teeth. A map is useful precisely because it is not the territory: it shrinks, flattens, selects, and throws nearly everything away. What survives is the thing you came for: structure you can use. Every scientific <Term as="em">model</Term><TipNote text={"A model is a purpose-built representation of a target system, simplified for a particular question or job."} /> performs the same trick. And once you see that, a harder question opens: when our best theories talk about things not observed in the ordinary direct way, but known through instruments, intervention, and inference, are they describing reality, or only drawing very good maps?
</Hero>

## The map is not the territory

The phrase belongs to Alfred Korzybski, who in 1933 was trying to cure a bad human habit: mistaking our words and symbols for reality itself. The full sentence matters:

<BlockQuote>
"A map is not the territory it represents, but, if correct, it has a similar structure to the territory, which accounts for its usefulness."
<Meta>Korzybski, *Science and Sanity*, 1933.</Meta>
</BlockQuote>

That clause about **similar structure** is the entire theory of modeling in miniature. A map is useless if it copies everything, as Borges saw, and useless if it copies nothing. It earns its keep in between, by preserving the relations that matter for a purpose while discarding the rest. A subway map is a triumph of distortion: distances are wrong, angles are wrong, rivers become cartoons. It keeps the order of stops and transfers, because that is what a rider needs.

<ImageFigure src={tabulaPeutingeriana.src} width="1800" height="453" alt="A cropped section of the Tabula Peutingeriana, a long Roman road map that stretches coastlines and cities into a route diagram.">
The Tabula Peutingeriana is not trying to be a scaled geographic copy. Its distortion preserves what travelers needed: roads, stations, and sequence.
</ImageFigure>

So a model is not a small true copy of the world. It is a **useful distortion**. Michael Weisberg's *Simulation and Similarity* gives the precise philosophical version: a model need not be identical to the target, or even perfectly isomorphic to it; it must be similar in the respects that matter, to the degree required by the job.

### All models are wrong

George E. P. Box gave statistics its most portable sentence: "All models are wrong, but some are useful." The citation trail is a miniature hype-filter lesson. His 1976 paper *Science and Statistics* contains the seed: because all models are wrong, the scientist cannot obtain a correct one by endless elaboration. The polished aphorism appears as a section heading in a 1979 conference chapter, and the familiar "essentially" wording lands in the 1987 textbook he wrote with Norman Draper.

Three dates, one idea sharpening over a decade. The slogan is real, but the casual citation often attached to it is not. Check the receipts; we will do that at industrial scale on **Day 149**.

<Aside>
<BlockTitle>A joke older than Borges</BlockTitle>

Borges was not first to the one-to-one map. In Lewis Carroll's *Sylvie and Bruno Concluded* (1893), a character boasts of a national map on the scale of "a mile to the mile." Why was it never unrolled? The farmers objected, because it would block out the sun and kill the crops. So they use the country itself as its own map. The territory is always its own most accurate model, and also the least useful one.
</Aside>

## The spherical cow, and why scientists lie on purpose

There is an old joke. A dairy farmer hires physicists to improve milk yield. Months later they return with a solution, but it works only for a **spherical cow in a vacuum**.

The joke is funny because it is true. Deliberately false assumptions, or <Term as="em">idealizations</Term><TipNote text={"An idealization is a disciplined distortion: a knowingly false simplification used to reveal, calculate, or explain something."} />, are not a bug in science; they are one of its sharpest tools. Galileo's law of falling bodies is clean only for bodies in a vacuum, with no air resistance, no spin, and an ideal gravitational field. None of that is exactly true. Ernan McMullin called this <Term as="em">Galilean idealization</Term><TipNote text={"Galilean idealization deliberately distorts a problem, often by removing complicating factors, so the core relation becomes mathematically tractable."} />: distort the problem to make the mechanism visible, then add detail back if the purpose demands it.

Sometimes the simplification is even deeper. A frictionless plane does not merely make the arithmetic easy; it reveals inertia by removing the thing that hides it. This <Term as="em">minimalist idealization</Term> is not an approximation of the world so much as an argument about what matters. The spherical cow, the ideal gas, the point mass, the infinite population in genetics, and the frictionless pulley all say: ignore this noise and watch the structure that remains.

"All models are wrong" is therefore not a counsel of despair. It is a counsel of focus. A model that tried to be right about everything would be Borges's map: total, faithful, and inert. The art is choosing which falsehoods to commit to.
</ContentSection>

## Do our maps describe a real world, or just work?

Here the practical question becomes one of philosophy's deepest fights. Physics speaks of electrons, quarks, fields, and spacetime curvature: entities and structures not observed in the ordinary direct way, but known through instruments, intervention, and inference. The theories work spectacularly. Does that success mean the unseen furniture is really there, more or less as described? Or are electrons entries in a powerful bookkeeping system, <Term as="em">empirically adequate</Term><TipNote text={"Empirical adequacy means getting the observable predictions right without committing to the hidden machinery as real."} /> without telling us what reality is like underneath?

The <Term as="em">scientific realist</Term> says success would be miraculous if our best theories were not latching onto reality. This is the no-miracles argument associated with Hilary Putnam and J. J. C. Smart: truth is the best explanation for sustained predictive success.

The anti-realist reply comes from history. Larry Laudan's 1981 paper *A Confutation of Convergent Realism* lists theory after theory that was successful in its day and false in ours:

- **Phlogiston**: the fire-stuff supposedly present in combustibles.
- **Caloric**: heat treated as an invisible fluid.
- **The luminiferous ether**: the medium light supposedly waved through.
- **Crystalline spheres**: the shells that carried the planets.
- **Vital forces**: the special spark of living matter.

The punch is brutal. Past success did not guarantee reference to real entities. Why assume our current success finally does? This is the <Term as="em">pessimistic meta-induction</Term><TipNote text={"The pessimistic meta-induction argues that because many successful past theories later proved false, current successful theories may also turn out false."} />. Add <Term as="em">underdetermination</Term><TipNote text={"Underdetermination is the problem that more than one theory can fit the same evidence, so evidence alone may not select a unique true theory."} /> and the realist has a serious problem: more than one map can fit the same ground.

### Four ways to live with the tension

Most philosophers do not camp at the poles. They ask a narrower question: after prediction succeeds, what license did you actually buy? The four positions below all respect working models. They disagree about whether successful maps license belief in approximate truth, surviving structure, manipulable entities, or only observable adequacy.

Read the table as a set of commitments, not as four slogans. Scientific realism is the first row's bold answer: sustained success is evidence that a theory has latched onto the world, at least approximately. Laudan's historical list is why that answer cannot be casual.

Structural realism, the second row, is the most map-like compromise. Look at the ether. Fresnel's ether disappeared; Maxwell's electromagnetic field replaced it. But parts of the mathematical structure survived the revolution. Maybe science accumulates structure more reliably than it accumulates furniture. Keep the equations; hold the entities loosely.

Entity realism, the third row, moves from prediction to intervention. Hacking's slogan was that if you can spray electrons, they are real. Believe in entities you can manipulate to do work; be more skeptical of the grand theory around them. Cartwright-style law skepticism supplies the complementary warning from *How the Laws of Physics Lie*: fundamental laws often govern idealized objects in carefully built models, not messy objects in the world.

Constructive empiricism, the final row, refuses the extra step. If a theory saves the observable phenomena, it has done its scientific job; belief in hidden entities is optional. That is why the next section matters. Computers are about to build maps that work astonishingly well while making every row of this table harder to keep tidy.
</ContentSection>

## The old fight, reborn in silicon

For most of history, science had two ways to learn: think about the world and poke the world. In the last few decades, a third has muscled in: **simulation**. A climate model runs a century in code. A jet engine's digital counterpart predicts a crack before it forms. On benchmark <Term as="em">hindcasts</Term><TipNote text={"A hindcast tests a forecast model by asking it to predict past events from earlier data, then comparing the forecast with what actually happened."} />, learned forecasters now beat leading physics-based baselines on some headline metrics. What kind of knowledge is that?

Simulation is not quite theory and not quite experiment. It inherits equations, approximations, data cleaning, numerical schemes, calibration habits, and institutional judgment. Eric Winsberg argues that simulations have their own epistemology: they can produce genuine knowledge, justified by practices that are not reducible to either pencil-and-paper theory or laboratory experiment. Paul Humphreys named the unease <Term as="em">epistemic opacity</Term><TipNote text={"Epistemic opacity is a situation where no human can survey all the relevant steps by which a computational result was produced."} />. A process may be formally specified and still too large for any human to inspect step by step. That opacity was already present in large simulations; neural models make it harder to ignore.

### When does a model earn the name?

<Term as="em">Digital twin</Term><TipNote text={"A strict digital twin has a virtual representation, live data from a physical counterpart, predictive use, and a bidirectional virtual-physical loop."} /> has become marketing language, so use the strict source: the 2024 U.S. National Academies report *Foundational Research Gaps and Future Directions for Digital Twins*. Under that bar, a twin is not merely a simulation. It is a virtual construct tied to a physical counterpart, updated by data from that counterpart, capable of prediction, and used to shape decisions about the system it represents. The feedback loop is the bar.

The hard problem is trust. The report emphasizes <Term as="em">VVUQ</Term><TipNote text={"VVUQ: verify code, validate model-world fit, and quantify uncertainty."} /> as a continual process, not a one-time stamp. The physical system changes, data change, decisions change, and the twin must be re-validated as it moves. That is also the report's anti-hype filter: separate a true bidirectional twin from an ordinary simulation, and separate an aspirational twin from one that actually exists.
</Claim>

### A digital twin of the whole planet

The most ambitious version is Europe's **Destination Earth** program, built with ECMWF, ESA, and EUMETSAT. On 10 June 2024, its first system release brought two high-priority twins online from the LUMI Supercomputer Center in Kajaani, Finland: Weather-Induced Extremes and Climate Change Adaptation. That matters because the phrase moved from policy vision to running infrastructure. The current Weather-Induced Extremes twin describes a 4.4 km global component, with 500-750 m regional European capability; earlier demonstrations and release materials used other km-scale configurations.

That is an established launch milestone, not a magic planet-copy. It is not yet the science-fiction object implied by the phrase "digital twin of Earth." Higher resolution can expose new structure, but it also raises hard questions about parameterization, data assimilation, verification, cost, and whether the extra detail improves the decision at hand. The European goal of a full Earth-system twin by 2030 remains a roadmap.
</Claim>

### Clinical twins: a narrow cousin that works

Medicine loves the idea of a digital twin of **you**: a running model of your body on which doctors could test interventions. The sober literature is far thinner. The National Academies report treats biomedical twins as a frontier with large research gaps, not a finished clinical platform. A 2025 scoping review in *npj Digital Medicine* found that only 18 of 149 healthcare studies using the label fully met the National Academies definition, and only two mentioned VVUQ.

The narrow cousin proves the rule. HeartFlow's FFR-CT takes a coronary CT scan and builds a patient-specific computational-fluid-dynamics model of blood flow to assess whether a blockage is starving the heart. It received FDA De Novo clearance in 2014. The scope is narrow, physical, and decision-specific: one organ system, one imaging moment, one clinical question. That is why it works. But it is not a clean full digital twin under the strict National Academies definition: the CT-derived model represents a patient at the time of imaging; it is not a dynamically updating, bidirectional virtual-physical loop. Its success shows how powerful a constrained patient-specific model can be, and why the full-body twin is a much larger claim. The broader FDA credibility framework points the same way: its 2023 guidance is for physics-based, mechanistic, or first-principles computational models in device submissions, not standalone machine-learning or AI models.
</Claim>

### Forecasting weather without hand-coding the forecast equations

For decades, weather forecasting meant solving atmospheric physics on enormous computers. Then machine-learning models trained on <Term as="em">reanalysis</Term><TipNote text={"Reanalysis reconstructs past weather by blending observations with a weather model into a physically consistent historical dataset."} /> and past weather caught up with, and on several benchmark hindcasts surpassed, leading physics-based systems. GraphCast, Pangu-Weather, and GenCast are not press-release curiosities; their headline results were published in *Science* and *Nature*. The qualifier matters: these are benchmark hindcasts and target metrics, not proof of universal operational superiority.
</Claim>

<Day10StaticFigure kind="weather-chart" locale="en" />

Read the three claims carefully. GraphCast reported outperforming a leading deterministic system on about 90% of 1,380 verification targets. GenCast reported greater skill than ECMWF's ensemble forecast on 97.2% of 1,320 targets. Pangu-Weather's headline is different: forecast-time and runtime gains against IFS-style baselines, not a share-of-targets percentage. The shared lesson is not that physics has been abolished. It is that learned maps can become astonishingly good at the task of forecasting before they become satisfying explanations.

The philosophical sting is sharp. By headline forecast-skill measures many users care about, these learned systems can be more empirically adequate than the systems built from explicit physical equations. Yet their interiors are opaque weights, not named mechanisms, and real users also care about uncertainty, calibration, extremes, robustness, and operational constraints. A constructive empiricist can shrug: prediction was the goal. A realist should worry more carefully: success alone is a less clean route to realism when the successful model is opaque and trained from prior model/data products.

The structural realist points to the hybrid response. Google's NeuralGCM keeps a differentiable atmospheric dynamics core and uses machine learning for parts the physics still parameterizes, approximates, or resolves only imperfectly. Keep the trusted structure; learn the closures. That is structural realism as engineering practice. But the caveat matters: like other learned systems, NeuralGCM is not expected to extrapolate far beyond its training regime. Mechanism may be exactly what you need at the edge of the map.

<SectionEyebrow>Open questions</SectionEyebrow>

## What's genuinely unsettled

- **Is simulation a third pillar of science, or applied math with better machines?** Winsberg and Margaret Morrison argue for a distinctive epistemology; critics still see expanded modeling.
- **Does predict-without-representing weaken scientific realism?** If a mechanism-free model can out-forecast a physical one, success alone looks less like evidence for truth.
- **Can learned models extrapolate?** The weather results are strongest inside regimes represented in training data. Novel climates and rare extremes are the empirical test.
- **When does a model deserve the name digital twin?** The National Academies drew a strict line. Many products using the phrase do not clear it.
- **What happens when the model is an AI system?** If a language model produces a true, well-supported claim, is it describing reality or only operating as an empirically adequate map? We return to this in the AI block.
</ContentSection>
